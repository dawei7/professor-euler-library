# Pure Mathematics

Learn to move from calculating examples to proving general statements about structures, spaces and functions. A proof is an argument that still holds when the example changes, and a counterexample is how you find the missing hypothesis.

**What you will learn**

Proof becomes a craft you can practice. A definition is the whole meaning of a word. A quantifier changes the claim: “for every ε there is a δ” is not “there is a δ for every ε”. Induction is a machine for the integers. A counterexample shows exactly which hypothesis was missing. You learn to write an argument that still holds when the numbers change.

Calculus becomes the theory of local linear approximation and of accumulation. The derivative at a point is the best linear guess. The integral adds those guesses up. The fundamental theorem says the two operations undo each other. Series ask when an infinite process has a sum, and Taylor’s theorem says how good a polynomial guess is, with a remainder you can bound. In several variables the derivative is a linear map, which is why linear algebra and calculus are one subject.

Linear algebra is learned twice, and both times count. Hefferon gives systems, row reduction, bases and matrices as concrete objects you can compute with. Axler then treats a linear map as the primary object: eigenvalues, inner products, and the spectral theorem, which says a self-adjoint operator is diagonal in some orthonormal basis. That theorem is why quadratic forms, and later covariance, can be rotated into independent axes.

Discrete mathematics and number theory train structure without continuity. Counting, recurrence and graphs are exact. Congruences and primes show the integers as a world with its own theorems: the Euclidean algorithm, unique factorization, and the fact that “divides” is a relation you can prove things about. Abstract algebra then names the pattern: groups for symmetry, rings for systems with two operations, fields for the scalars linear algebra needs.

Analysis is calculus with the hypotheses restored. Completeness of the reals is the axiom that makes limits exist. Continuity, the mean value theorem and the Riemann integral become theorems with proofs. Metric spaces and several variables follow: compactness is the hypothesis that forces a continuous function to attain its maximum. Topology then drops the metric. An open set is the primitive, and a continuous function is one whose preimage of an open set is open.

The advanced common foundation is three different kinds of infinity. Complex analysis is rigid: a holomorphic function is determined by its values on a small disc. Cauchy’s theorem and the residue theorem turn a contour integral into local data at the singularities, and a holomorphic function that is not identically zero has isolated zeros. If those zeros accumulate inside the domain, the function is zero everywhere. Measure theory rebuilds the integral so that limits and integrals trade places under honest hypotheses, and Lᵖ spaces appear. Functional analysis is linear algebra in infinite dimensions, where completeness matters: Banach and Hilbert spaces, bounded operators, and the spectral ideas of Axler returning as theorems about operators.

**Why this order works**

Hammack is first because the later books speak in quantifiers. “For every ε > 0 there exists δ > 0” is unreadable if ∀ and ∃ are still new, and induction is the tool Lebl, Judson and Raji will all assume. Sets, functions as sets of pairs, and cardinality are also here, so that “injection” and “the reals are uncountable” are already English.

Calculus Volumes 1, 2 and 3 come early, with Hefferon between the first and the second. You want a large stock of examples before the abstract books sort them. The derivative as a linear approximation is the reason Axler will feel necessary: in several variables, the derivative at a point is a matrix. Series and Taylor theorems are the first place an infinite process has an error term. Hefferon before that multivariable calculus means you can already row-reduce and talk about a basis when the derivative becomes a linear map.

Levin sits in the middle of the calculus sequence on purpose. Discrete mathematics is a source of exact structures. Counting and graphs stay exact, and induction gets a second life. When probability arrives later, you will already know how to count.

Axler waits until after Hefferon and after multivariable calculus. A second linear algebra that starts from operators, and that postpones determinants, pays off after you have solved linear systems with your hands and seen a derivative that is a linear map. The spectral theorem is the summit of that second pass.

Lebl’s first volume comes before abstract algebra and before the return to probability. Real analysis is where completeness, continuity and the Riemann integral are proved. Judson and Raji then have a reader who can sustain a long argument. Groups, rings and fields name patterns you have already met: invertible matrices form a group, polynomials form a ring, and the scalars of a vector space form a field. Elementary number theory is the integers seen with those eyes: divisibility, congruences, primes.

Grinstead and Snell come after you can count and after you can integrate. Expectation is a linear functional on random variables. The law of large numbers is an analytic statement about averages. Probability sits here, after counting and integration, so it arrives as mathematics rather than as a bag of formulas.

Differential equations and numerical analysis are the applied guests on a pure path, and they earn the visit. The solution set of a linear differential equation is a vector space, so the course is linear algebra plus calculus. Numerical analysis is what you do when a theorem guarantees a number you cannot write down: truncation, conditioning, and the difference between an exact identity and a computed approximation.

Lebl’s second volume, Badzioch’s topology and Hitchman’s geometry then build the language of spaces. Metric spaces make “close” precise. Compactness is the hypothesis behind extreme-value theorems. Topology removes the metric and keeps continuity. Geometry after that can talk about curvature and about the shape of a universe, including spaces that are not subsets of the plane.

Complex analysis and measure theory are independent branches at this point in the path. Holomorphic functions do not need Lebesgue integration. Read Lebl’s complex book when you want rigidity, residues and the fact that one derivative implies infinitely many. Read Axler’s measure book when you want an integral that survives limits. Functional analysis comes after the measure book, because Lᵖ spaces and Hilbert space are built on that integral, and because an infinite-dimensional operator needs a complete space. Axler’s finite-dimensional spectral theorem is the story this last book tells again, with hypotheses.

**How to study**

Keep a proof notebook: state the claim, list the hypotheses, try examples and counterexamples, then write the argument in your own words. Reconstruct selected proofs after closing the book. Use the advanced common foundation as a starting point for further reading in the topics that interest you.
