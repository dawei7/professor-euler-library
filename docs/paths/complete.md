# Complete Mathematics

Build a broad mathematical education that connects proof, computation, uncertainty and learning. The same idea often returns in a second language: an integral becomes a measure, a matrix becomes an operator, and a fitted model becomes a statistical claim.

**What you will learn**

You meet the same idea in several languages, which is the real training of a broad path. A derivative begins as a slope, becomes the best linear approximation, and in several variables is a linear map, which is a matrix once a basis is chosen. An integral begins as an area, becomes a limit of sums, and in the measure book becomes an integral that can trade places with a limit under a named theorem. A matrix begins as a table you row-reduce, becomes a linear map, and in functional analysis becomes a bounded operator on a complete infinite-dimensional space. A fitted model begins as a formula, becomes a statistical claim with bias and variance, and ends as a question about approximation, optimization and generalization.

Algebra and geometry train the eye for structure. School algebra is the grammar of expressions, domains and functions. School geometry is the first proof about a picture. Trigonometry puts the triangle on the unit circle, and radians are the unit in which the derivative of sine is cosine. Precalculus weaves polynomials, rational functions, exponentials, logarithms and periodic functions into one subject. Later, groups name symmetry, rings name systems with two operations, and fields are the scalars a vector space is allowed to use. Topology drops the metric and keeps continuity: a function is continuous when the preimage of an open set is open. Geometry returns at the end of the undergraduate breadth as the shape of a space, including spaces that are not subsets of the plane.

Computation and uncertainty train judgment about error. Truncation error is the piece of a Taylor expansion you discarded. Conditioning is a property of the problem, stability a property of the algorithm, convergence the theorem that the error tends to zero as the mesh or the step is refined. Probability is a model of variation. Statistics is the logic of what a sample entitles you to say. A stochastic process is that model with a memory. Optimization is how a constrained problem becomes a procedure, and a deep network is a composition of maps trained by such a procedure. High-dimensional statistics is the correction to low-dimensional intuition: in a thousand coordinates, distance behaves differently than it does in the plane.

The advanced common foundation is three kinds of infinity, and the last ten books spend it. Complex analysis is rigid, one derivative implying infinitely many, with contour integrals reduced to residues. Measure theory rebuilds volume so that the integral survives limits. Functional analysis is linear algebra in those spaces of functions. Partial differential equations, numerical methods, convex optimization, rigorous probability, inference, stochastic processes, deep learning, learning theory and high-dimensional statistics are what a person with that foundation can then read as one library.

**Why this order works**

This path follows the shelf. School books S.01 through S.04, then books 01 through 30 in numerical order. That is a traversal of the whole English library. It is a good order for someone who wants the breadth itself. Adjacent numbers are often cousins. The focused paths exist because a destination sometimes wants a different sequence. The interesting part of this path is knowing where those sequences disagree, and why.

The school block is the one place the order is forced. Wallace’s algebra is the grammar. CK-12’s geometry gives you proofs about figures and the coordinate plane, so that Pythagoras is both a picture and a theorem. Corral’s trigonometry lifts triangles to the unit circle and installs radians before any derivative of sine appears. Stitz–Zeager weaves the earlier books into functions: polynomials, rational functions and their asymptotes, exponentials and logarithms as inverses, trigonometric functions as periodic functions, sequences as functions on the positive integers. The overlap is deliberate. If this block is already fluent, the university foundation is the start.

Books 01 through 09 are the shared undergraduate language, in an order that favors examples before abstraction. Hammack installs quantifiers, sets, induction and functions as sets of pairs. Calculus Volume 1 then has a reader who can say “for every ε there is a δ”. Hefferon puts linear systems, bases and matrices in your hands before Calculus Volume 2 asks for series and Taylor’s theorem with remainder. Levin’s discrete mathematics sits between those calculus volumes: counting, graphs and a second life for induction, while the analysis is still concrete. Calculus Volume 3 makes the derivative in several variables a linear map, which is why the linear algebra was part of the same story. Axler’s second course then treats the linear map as the primary object and reaches the spectral theorem. Grinstead and Snell, then modern statistics, give expectation and the logic of a sample while the integral is still the calculus integral.

Books 10 through 20 are where the same ideas grow hypotheses. Lebl’s first analysis volume rebuilds the real line: completeness, continuity, the mean value theorem, the Riemann integral. Judson names the patterns as groups, rings and fields. Raji treats the integers as their own theory: divisibility, the Euclidean algorithm, congruences, primes. Differential equations and Brin’s numerical analysis are the applied guests. A linear differential equation has a vector space of solutions. A numerical method is what you do when a theorem guarantees a number you cannot write in closed form. Lebl’s second volume does metric spaces and several variables. Compactness is the hypothesis that forces a continuous function on a closed bounded set to attain its maximum. Badzioch drops the metric. Hitchman returns to geometry with topology available. Lebl’s complex book, Axler’s measure book and Rodríguez’s functional analysis are the advanced common foundation, and here the numerical order is a sensible one: holomorphic functions do not need Lebesgue theory, and functional analysis wants the measure in place because Lᵖ and Hilbert space are built on that integral.

Books 21 through 30 spend the foundation, and here the shelf order and the focused paths part company on purpose. Speck’s partial differential equations come first: heat, wave and Laplace as equations. Johnson’s numerical methods follow as algorithms with errors you can name. The numerical PDE course then turns a continuous equation into a grid. Suh’s convex optimization comes after that, because on this shelf optimization is book 24. Polyanskiy rebuilds probability on the measure you already read as book 19. Inference and Gallager’s stochastic processes follow. Prince, Bach, and Samworth–Shah close the shelf: modern neural networks, then learning theory (approximation, optimization, generalization, including overparameterized models), then nonparametric and high-dimensional statistics.

Compare that with the other paths before you copy the shelf blindly. The applied path pulls probability and statistics forward, ahead of differential equations, because an applied model is often a deterministic equation plus noise, and it postpones the second linear algebra and the real analysis until a first numerical course has made convergence feel necessary. It also places Suh immediately before the numerical PDE course, as breadth for constrained models, and it makes Polyanskiy, inference and Gallager an optional extension. The AI path keeps Levin, because learning theory counts, and it places Suh after Gallager and immediately before Prince, so that gradient descent and duality are already mathematics when a network appears. Prince before Bach is deliberate there: the theory is sharper with a model in view. This complete path visits those books in numerical order instead, which means you will meet optimization before the measure-theoretic probability sequence, and a network only at book 28. The shelf’s virtue is that nothing is skipped and the map stays easy to remember. The focused paths’ virtue is that each tool arrives in the season you need it.

**How to study**

Treat this as a long-term map. Work in stages, revisit important ideas in a second setting, and use projects to connect subjects. You do not need to finish every book before applying what you know. If you already have the entry skills, begin at the university foundation.
