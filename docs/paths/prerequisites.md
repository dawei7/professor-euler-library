# Algebra, Geometry & Precalculus

Build the algebra, geometry and function skills that make university mathematics feel approachable. Later proofs and models are written in equations, shapes and functions, so this is the shared entry unless you already have that fluency.

**What you will learn**

Algebra is the grammar. An equation is a claim that two expressions name the same number, and a legal step is a reversible operation. You learn to keep the domain in sight: a square root hides a sign, a denominator forbids a root, a logarithm demands a positive argument. Factoring is the statement that a polynomial knows its roots, and that a product is zero only when a factor is zero.

A function is an object, not a formula you plug a number into and forget. You can add functions, compose them, invert them when they are one-to-one, and read their graphs as geometry. A horizontal line that meets the graph twice is a picture of a failed inverse: the function takes the same value twice, so no inverse exists. Shifting, stretching and reflecting a graph is the same algebra acting on the plane.

Geometry is the first place a proof is about a picture you can still be wrong about. Congruence and similarity say when two shapes are the same figure in different clothes. Circles carry inscribed angles, and coordinates put those theorems back into algebra: the distance formula is Pythagoras, and a line is a linear equation. The Pythagorean theorem is worth knowing both as a picture and as a proof.

Trigonometry lifts triangles onto the circle. Sine and cosine are coordinates of a point on the unit circle, so the identity cos²θ + sin²θ = 1 is Pythagoras, not a formula to memorize. Radians are the honest unit, because the derivative of sin θ is cos θ only when θ is in radians. Inverse trig functions exist only after you restrict the domain. The law of sines and the law of cosines are how a triangle that is not right-angled still yields its sides.

Precalculus is where those languages share a page. Polynomials, rational functions and their asymptotes, exponentials and logarithms as inverses, trigonometric functions as periodic functions, and sequences as discrete cousins of functions. Composition and inverse functions are the habits calculus will use every day. A limit, when you meet one, will be a question about a function you already know how to hold.

### Entry foundations

Progress from manipulating expressions to interpreting shapes and functions. This is the shared entry route for the English university paths, unless you already have equivalent knowledge.

1. **S.01 — Beginning and Intermediate Algebra** — Tyler Wallace
2. **S.02 — Geometry, with matching Second Edition key** — CK-12 Foundation
3. **S.03 — Trigonometry** — Michael E. Corral
4. **S.04 — Precalculus** — Carl Stitz and Jeff Zeager

**Why this order works**

Wallace comes first because every later page in the library is written in algebraic grammar. Solving, factoring, functions, exponents and logarithms are the moves inside a derivative, a matrix row reduction and a probability calculation.

Geometry comes next, before trigonometry. You want the triangle theorems, congruence and similarity as things you can prove, and you want coordinates so that a picture and an equation are the same object. Corral then has something to lift. Trigonometry that starts from the unit circle is the version calculus can differentiate. Identities become algebra on sine and cosine. Radians are introduced here so that the chain rule later does not have to fight a constant from degrees.

Stitz–Zeager is last on purpose. It assumes the algebra, the geometry and the trigonometry, and it weaves them: a trigonometric function is a function, its inverse has a restricted domain, a rational function has a graph with asymptotes, and a sequence is a function on the positive integers. The overlap with Wallace and Corral is useful. Meeting a logarithm twice, once as an inverse and once inside a precalculus problem, is how the idea sticks.

Nothing here is optional decoration for the university paths. If these four are already fluent, start later. If a later book feels slippery, the slip is often a function, a radian, or a domain, and the repair is back here.

**How to study**

Start with ordinary arithmetic fluency. Write your own solution before opening an answer. Move on when you can explain the method and solve unfamiliar examples; use later books to revisit earlier gaps.

**Companions**

No extra companion is required. The worked examples and available source answers in these four books support independent practice. Boyd and Vandenberghe's applied linear algebra, linked from Applied Mathematics, is a later personal-download book about vectors and least squares. It assumes this entry work, and it does not replace these four books.
