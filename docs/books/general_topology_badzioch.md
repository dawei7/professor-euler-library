## What this book trains

Bernard Badzioch’s Introduction to Topology I: General Topology takes the open set as the primitive notion. A topology is a family of sets closed under arbitrary unions and finite intersections. Continuity is no longer an ε–δ statement. A function is continuous when the preimage of every open set is open. Connectedness, path connectedness, the separation axioms, and compactness are the properties that survive when there is no distance.

Metric spaces are still here, at the beginning, because they are the source of the intuition and because several great theorems say when a topological space comes from a metric. The Urysohn lemma, the Tietze extension theorem, and the Urysohn metrization theorem are that conversation. The Heine–Borel theorem returns compactness of closed bounded sets in Euclidean space as a special case. Tychonoff’s theorem says a product of compact spaces is compact. Quotient spaces, simplicial complexes, embeddings of manifolds and mapping spaces open the door toward algebraic topology and geometry without walking through it.

## Why it is worth knowing

Topology is the study of properties that a continuous deformation does not change. Connectedness is why the intermediate value theorem is true, once you say it as “the continuous image of a connected set is connected.” Compactness is why a continuous function on a closed bounded set attains its bounds, once you say it as “the continuous image of a compact set is compact.” The metric was a convenient way to talk. The topological statement is the one that still makes sense on a surface, on a space of functions, or on a quotient.

The joy is the precision of the separation axioms. Hausdorff, regular, normal: each forbids a slightly subtler way for points and closed sets to be inseparable. Urysohn’s lemma says that in a normal space two disjoint closed sets can be separated by a continuous function to the interval. That function is how you build distances, and it is why metrization is a theorem rather than a definition. Tychonoff’s theorem is famously equivalent to the axiom of choice. Using it is a mathematical event, and the book is where you see the statement earn its keep.

## What it asks of the other books

Hammack (01) for sets, functions, images and preimages, and cardinality. Lebl I (10) for metric spaces, open and closed sets, completeness and compactness in the metric setting. Lebl II (15) for the several-variable analysis that makes you want a language beyond the metric. Badzioch’s first mathematical chapters are some set theory and metric spaces, so the Lebl metric chapter and this opening overlap on purpose.

Hitchman’s geometry (17) uses the topological language on surfaces and on the shape of a universe. Functional analysis (20) uses completeness and compactness in infinite-dimensional spaces. You do not need measure theory or complex analysis before this book. The pure path places it after Lebl II and before Hitchman.

## A walk through the chapters

### 1. After the title page, Some Set Theory and Metric Spaces

After the title page, Some Set Theory and Metric Spaces reset the language. Open Sets, Basis, Subbasis, Subspace, and Closed Sets then build a topology from its open sets, from a basis, or by inheriting a topology on a subset. A basis is a small family of open sets that generates the rest by unions. A subbasis generates by finite intersections and then unions. Subspace topology is how a surface sitting in space gets its open sets from the space around it.

### 2. Continuous Functions

Continuous Functions. The preimage definition, and the consequences you already know in metric spaces: compositions of continuous functions are continuous, and restrictions behave. This is the definition Hammack’s image-and-preimage section was preparing.

### 3. Connectedness, and Path Connectedness

Connectedness, and Path Connectedness. A space is connected when it is not the union of two disjoint nonempty open sets. Path connectedness is stronger: any two points are joined by a continuous path. The two agree on open subsets of Euclidean space and disagree in clever examples. The intermediate value theorem is connectedness of an interval, pushed forward by a continuous function.

### 4. Separation Axioms, the Urysohn Lemma, the Tietze Extension Theorem, the Urysohn Metrization Theorem, and Metrization of Manifolds

Separation Axioms, the Urysohn Lemma, the Tietze Extension Theorem, the Urysohn Metrization Theorem, and Metrization of Manifolds. Separation is about whether points and closed sets can be placed in disjoint open neighborhoods. Urysohn’s lemma produces a continuous real-valued function that is 0 on one closed set and 1 on another. Tietze says a continuous function already defined on a closed subset of a normal space extends to the whole space. Metrization says which spaces are secretly metric spaces. The manifold case is the one geometry cares about: a space that locally looks like Euclidean space, and when that space can carry a global distance.

### 5. Compact Spaces, the Heine–Borel Theorem, Compact Metric Spaces, Tychonoff’s Theorem, and Compactification

Compact Spaces, the Heine–Borel Theorem, Compact Metric Spaces, Tychonoff’s Theorem, and Compactification. Compactness is the open-cover definition. Heine–Borel says the compact subsets of Euclidean space are the closed bounded ones, so the calculus fact and the topological fact are the same fact. Compact metric spaces recover sequential compactness. Tychonoff’s theorem is the product. A compactification adds points so that a noncompact space sits densely inside a compact one. The one-point compactification of the line is a circle. That picture is worth the definition.

### 6. Quotient Spaces, Simplicial Complexes, Embeddings of Manifolds, and Mapping Spaces

Quotient Spaces, Simplicial Complexes, Embeddings of Manifolds, and Mapping Spaces. A quotient identifies points. A simplicial complex is a space built by gluing edges, triangles and higher simplices along faces. An embedding puts one space inside another without tearing. A mapping space is a space whose points are functions. These four chapters are the exit toward algebraic topology, geometric topology and analysis on function spaces. The book remains a first course in general topology. It equips those exits rather than taking them.
