# S.04 Precalculus

*Carl Stitz and Jeff Zeager*

## What this book trains

Precalculus, by Carl Stitz and Jeff Zeager, is where algebra, geometry and trigonometry share a page and become one subject: functions. Polynomials, rational functions and their asymptotes, exponentials and logarithms as inverses, trigonometric functions as functions on the real line, sequences as functions on the positive integers, and vectors as the first objects with both length and direction.

The book also tells you the structural facts that school algebra usually only uses. The factor theorem and the remainder theorem say what a root knows about a polynomial. The fundamental theorem of algebra says a polynomial of degree n has n roots in the complex numbers, counted properly. Conic sections are the curves a quadratic equation cuts from the plane. Matrices and Gaussian elimination appear as a way to organize a linear system, which is a preview of Hefferon and not a replacement.

Induction and the binomial theorem sit in the sequences chapter. They are the first infinite processes with a proof, and they are why Hammack’s counting and induction chapters will feel like a return rather than a beginning.

## Why it is worth knowing

Calculus is the study of functions you already know how to hold. A limit is a question about a function. A derivative is the best linear guess at a function. If the function itself is still a stranger, the limit has nothing to hold onto. Stitz–Zeager is the book that makes the stranger familiar: you can shift it, stretch it, invert it, compose it, and read its asymptotes.

The usefulness is concrete. Rational functions are the shapes of many physical response curves. Exponential models are growth and decay. Sinusoids are signals. A partial fraction decomposition, prepared here and completed in Calculus Volume 2, turns a rational function into a sum that can be integrated term by term. Gaussian elimination is the algorithm inside every later linear system, from a resistor network to a least-squares fit.

## What it asks of the other books

Wallace (S.01) for algebraic fluency. The opening chapter of Stitz–Zeager reviews sets, linear and absolute value equations, polynomials, factoring, quadratics, rational expressions, radicals and complex numbers, so gaps can be repaired in place. CK-12 (S.02) for the coordinate plane and the geometric meaning of a circle and a distance. Corral (S.03) for trigonometry. The trigonometry chapters here are a second, deeper pass, from the unit circle through polar form and vectors. The overlap is useful.

Calculus Volume 1 starts from this book’s functions. Hefferon starts from this book’s linear systems and goes much further: vector spaces, maps, determinants, eigenvalues. Hammack will replace the short induction section with a theory of proof. Levin will replace the binomial theorem’s combinatorial hint with a counting course.

## A walk through the chapters

### 1. Prerequisites

Prerequisites is an honest repair shop. Basic set theory and interval notation, real arithmetic, linear equations and inequalities, absolute value, polynomial arithmetic, factoring, quadratics, rational expressions and equations, radicals, and complex numbers. A reader who is fluent can move quickly. A reader who is not should stay. The rest of the book quotes these moves without pausing.

### 2. Relations and Functions

Relations and Functions. The Cartesian plane, relations, functions, function notation, function arithmetic, graphs, and transformations. A relation is any set of pairs. A function is a relation that assigns one output to each allowed input. Shifting, stretching and reflecting a graph is the algebra of the plane acting on a picture. This is the chapter that makes “f” a noun.

### 3. Linear and Quadratic Functions

Linear and Quadratic Functions studies the two families you can draw completely. Lines, absolute value functions, parabolas, inequalities involving them, and regression, which is the first time a line is chosen to fit data rather than to satisfy an equation exactly. Regression here is a preview of least squares. Hefferon has a topic section, Line of Best Fit, that explains the linear algebra underneath.

### 4. Polynomial Functions

Polynomial Functions. Graphs, the factor and remainder theorems, real zeros, and complex zeros with the fundamental theorem of algebra. A root corresponds to a linear factor. Over the complex numbers the factorization is complete. That theorem is one of the reasons complex numbers earn their place before anyone studies complex analysis.

### 5. Rational Functions

Rational Functions. The definition, the graphs, asymptotes and holes, and rational inequalities with applications. A vertical asymptote is a domain failure you can see. A horizontal or slant asymptote is the polynomial part winning at infinity. Calculus will compute those end behaviors with limits.

### 6. Further Topics in Functions

Further Topics in Functions. Composition, inverse functions, and other algebraic functions. Composition is the operation the chain rule differentiates. An inverse function undoes another function, and it exists precisely when the original function is one-to-one. Restricting the domain to create an inverse is the same move Corral made for arcsine.

### 7. Exponential and Logarithmic Functions

Exponential and Logarithmic Functions. The two families as inverses, the properties of logarithms, exponential and logarithmic equations and inequalities, and applications. A logarithm turns a product into a sum. That is why it linearizes growth, and why it appears in entropy and in information once probability is a subject. The applications here are the concrete ones: growth, decay, intensity, interest.

### 8. Hooked on Conics

Hooked on Conics. Circles, parabolas, ellipses and hyperbolas as geometric definitions and as quadratic equations. A parabola is the set of points equidistant from a focus and a directrix. An ellipse is a constant sum of distances to two foci. The names return in the polar chapter, and the pictures return when quadratic forms are diagonalized in linear algebra.

### 9. Systems of Equations and Matrices

Systems of Equations and Matrices. Gaussian elimination, augmented matrices, matrix arithmetic, inverses, determinants and Cramer’s rule, partial fraction decomposition, and nonlinear systems. This is a working introduction, not the theory. You learn to organize coefficients into a rectangle and to eliminate. Hefferon begins where this chapter stops asking “how do I compute?” and starts asking “what is the set of solutions, as a vector space?”

### 10. Sequences and the Binomial Theorem

Sequences and the Binomial Theorem. Sequences, summation notation, mathematical induction, and the binomial theorem. A sequence is a function on the positive integers. Induction is the proof that follows it from one index to the next. The binomial theorem expands a power of a sum, and its coefficients count subsets. Calculus Volume 2 will ask when an infinite version of a sum has a value. Hammack will make induction a craft.

### 11. Foundations of Trigonometry, then Applications of Trigonometry

Foundations of Trigonometry, then Applications of Trigonometry. Angles and their measure, the unit circle, the six circular functions, identities, graphs, inverses, and equations. Then sinusoids, the law of sines, the law of cosines, polar coordinates and their graphs, the conics again in polar form, the polar form of complex numbers, vectors, the dot product and projection, and parametric equations. A vector and a dot product are the last new objects. Projection is the geometric meaning of a dot product, and it is the seed of least squares, of Fourier coefficients, and of the orthogonal projection Hefferon constructs with Gram–Schmidt.
