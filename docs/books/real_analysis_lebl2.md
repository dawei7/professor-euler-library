## What this book trains

Jiří Lebl’s Basic Analysis II is several variables, done with the proofs Volume 3 did not carry, and then the theorems about limits of functions that point toward complex analysis and toward topology. The derivative of a map between vector spaces is a bounded linear map. Continuity of that derivative yields the inverse and implicit function theorems. Higher-order derivatives, differentiation under the integral sign, path integrals and path independence, the multivariable Riemann integral, Fubini, outer measure and null sets, Jordan measurable sets, Green’s theorem, and change of variables.

The last chapter treats functions as limits. Power series and analytic functions, the complex exponential and trigonometric functions, the maximum principle and the fundamental theorem of algebra, equicontinuity and Arzelà–Ascoli, the Stone–Weierstrass theorem, and Fourier series. Complex numbers are introduced here as a tool, not as the complex-analysis course. That course is Lebl’s Guide to Cultivating Complex Analysis (18).

## Why it is worth knowing

The inverse function theorem is the several-variable sentence you have been approaching since the chain rule. If the derivative at a point is an invertible linear map, the function itself is locally invertible, and the derivative of the inverse is the inverse of the derivative. The implicit function theorem is the same fact when the equation does not present one variable as a function of the others. Constrained optimization and the change-of-variables formula both rest on it.

Arzelà–Ascoli and Stone–Weierstrass are the compactness theorems for spaces of functions. A bounded equicontinuous family has a uniformly convergent subsequence. Polynomials are dense in the continuous functions on a compact interval, in the uniform norm. Those two theorems are why a sequence of approximating functions can be trusted to produce a function, which is the analytic form of every series solution and every Fourier expansion you have already used. Green’s theorem returns from Volume 3 with hypotheses written down.

## What it asks of the other books

Lebl I (10) for the real line, uniform convergence, the Riemann integral and metric spaces. Calculus Volume 3 (06) for the pictures: partial derivatives, multiple integrals, Green’s theorem. Hefferon (03) or Axler (07) for vector spaces and linear maps. The opening section of this volume reviews vector spaces, linear mappings and convexity, and analysis with vector spaces. Axler makes the derivative-as-linear-map language native. Hefferon is enough to begin.

Topology (16) takes open sets further. Complex analysis (18) takes analytic functions further. The measure book (19) replaces outer measure and the Riemann integral with Lebesgue’s theory. This volume is the common predecessor. Fourier series here connect back to Lebl’s differential equations (13).

## A walk through the chapters

### 1. Several Variables and Partial Derivatives

Several Variables and Partial Derivatives. Vector spaces, linear mappings and convexity, analysis in vector spaces, the derivative, continuity and the derivative, the inverse and implicit function theorems, and higher-order derivatives. Convexity is planted here because a convex set is the geometric hypothesis optimization will want, and because a ball in a normed space is convex. The derivative is defined by a limit of a linear approximation, the same definition as the tangent plane, now a theorem about maps of normed spaces. Equality of mixed partials returns with its continuity hypothesis.

### 2. One-dimensional Integrals in Several Variables

One-dimensional Integrals in Several Variables. Differentiation under the integral sign, path integrals, and path independence. Differentiation under the integral is an interchange theorem, the theme of Volume I’s sequences of functions, now with a parameter. A path integral of a gradient depends only on the endpoints. That is the conservative-field theorem of Volume 3, proved in the language of this book.

### 3. Multivariable Integral

Multivariable Integral. The Riemann integral over rectangles, iterated integrals and Fubini’s theorem, outer measure and null sets, which functions are Riemann integrable, Jordan measurable sets, Green’s theorem, and change of variables. A null set is a set that can be covered by rectangles of arbitrarily small total volume. Riemann integrability can be told in those terms. Jordan measure is the volume theory that matches the Riemann integral. It is not yet Lebesgue measure. The change of variables formula multiplies by the absolute value of the Jacobian determinant, the fact Volume 3 used and this chapter proves.

### 4. Functions as Limits

Functions as Limits. Complex numbers, swapping limits, power series and analytic functions, the complex exponential and trigonometric functions, the maximum principle and the fundamental theorem of algebra, equicontinuity and Arzelà–Ascoli, Stone–Weierstrass, and Fourier series. An analytic function equals its power series. The maximum principle says a complex-analytic function on a bounded region attains its maximum modulus on the boundary, and the fundamental theorem of algebra is a consequence. Meeting it here, after Stitz–Zeager stated it and after polynomials were factored in algebra, is the proof joining the courses. Fourier series close the volume by returning a concrete expansion to the general approximation theorems.
