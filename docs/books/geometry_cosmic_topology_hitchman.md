## What this book trains

Michael P. Hitchman’s Geometry with an Introduction to Cosmic Topology is geometry after Euclid’s parallel postulate has been allowed to vary. The complex plane, inversions and Möbius transformations are the maps. Euclidean, hyperbolic and elliptic geometry are three members of one family, distinguished by curvature. Surfaces and quotient spaces are how a local geometry becomes a global shape. Cosmic topology asks which of those shapes a three-dimensional universe could have, and what a sky full of repeated circles would mean.

Euclid’s postulates are revisited after the elliptic chapter, when you can see which postulate was the hinge. The parallel postulate is independent of the others. Hyperbolic geometry, in the Poincaré disk and in the upper half-plane, is the model where through a point not on a line there are many parallels. Elliptic geometry, built from antipodal points on a sphere, is the model where there are none.

## Why it is worth knowing

A Möbius transformation is a function (az + b) / (cz + d) of the complex plane, and the group of those functions is the symmetry group of the geometries in this book. Circles and lines trade places under inversion. Angles are preserved. Once that group feels natural, hyperbolic geometry stops being a list of strange theorems and becomes the geometry of a disk with a different ruler.

Curvature is the number that organizes the family. Positive curvature is elliptic, zero is Euclidean, negative is hyperbolic. Area of a triangle in the non-Euclidean cases is determined by its angles. That is the shock: the angle sum is not π, and the difference measures the area. Quotient spaces, prepared in Badzioch and used here, glue a polygon’s edges to make a surface. A torus is a square with opposite edges identified. Cosmic crystallography and circles in the sky are the observational form of the same gluing, asked of the universe rather than of a textbook polygon.

## What it asks of the other books

Complex numbers and polar form from Corral (S.03), Stitz–Zeager (S.04), or Lebl II (15). The complex-plane chapter reviews basic notions, polar form, division and angle measure. Trigonometry is assumed. Euclidean geometry from CK-12 (S.02) is the geometry being generalized. Badzioch (16) for open sets, continuity and quotient spaces. The book can be read when those words are young. The quotient chapter will land harder after topology.

Lebl’s complex analysis (18) shares Möbius transformations, there called linear fractional transformations, and the disk. Hitchman uses them as symmetries of a geometry. Lebl uses them as the automorphisms of a domain. Either order teaches you something the other will quote. The pure path places Hitchman after topology and before complex analysis.

## A walk through the chapters

### 1. An Invitation to Geometry

An Invitation to Geometry. An introduction, a brief history, and a first look at geometry on surfaces. The history matters because the parallel postulate was a sore point for two thousand years before the other geometries were accepted as geometries. The first look at surfaces is a promise the later chapter keeps.

### 2. The Complex Plane

The Complex Plane. Basic notions, polar form, division and angle measure, and complex expressions. Division by a complex number is multiplication by the conjugate over the modulus squared, and the argument of a quotient is a difference of angles. That is the whole arithmetic the transformations need.

### 3. Transformations

Transformations. Basic transformations of the complex plane, inversion, the extended plane, and Möbius transformations in two passes. The extended plane adds the point at infinity so that inversion and a Möbius map are defined everywhere they morally should be. Inversion in a circle sends the inside to the outside and fixes the circle. It preserves angles and sends circles and lines to circles and lines.

### 4. Geometry, then Hyperbolic Geometry

Geometry, then Hyperbolic Geometry. The basics, Möbius geometry, the Poincaré disk, figures, measurement, area and triangle trigonometry, and the upper half-plane model. Two models of the same hyperbolic geometry are related by a Möbius transformation. A geodesic is a diameter or a circular arc meeting the boundary at right angles, in the disk. Distance grows without bound as you approach the boundary, so the boundary is infinitely far away. Triangle trigonometry in this metric is a cousin of Corral’s laws, with hyperbolic functions where cosines stood.

### 5. Elliptic Geometry

Elliptic Geometry. Antipodal points, the geometry itself, measurement, and a return to Euclid’s postulates. Identifying antipodal points on a sphere makes a projective plane in which any two lines meet. There is no parallel. Euclid’s postulates can now be sorted into the ones every geometry in the book keeps and the one that chose the curvature.

### 6. Geometry on Surfaces

Geometry on Surfaces. Curvature, elliptic geometry with positive curvature, hyperbolic geometry with negative curvature, the family of geometries (Xₖ, Gₖ), surfaces, the geometry of surfaces, and quotient spaces. The family (Xₖ, Gₖ) is the book’s unification: a space Xₖ and a group Gₖ of allowed motions, one for each curvature k. A surface inherits a geometry. A quotient by a group of motions can make a compact surface from a piece of the model, which is the construction cosmic topology will use one dimension higher.

### 7. Cosmic Topology

Cosmic Topology. Three-dimensional geometry and 3-manifolds, cosmic crystallography, circles in the sky, and our universe. A 3-manifold is a space that locally looks like ordinary space. If the universe is a quotient of a three-dimensional geometry, patterns can repeat. Crystallography looks for those repeats in the distribution of objects. Circles in the sky look for matched circles in the cosmic microwave background. The chapter is careful about what would count as evidence. The list of symbols, references and index close the book.
