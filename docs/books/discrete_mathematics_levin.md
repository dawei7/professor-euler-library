*Oscar Levin*

## What this book trains

Oscar Levin’s Discrete Mathematics: An Open Introduction is the mathematics of exact finite structures. Logic and proofs, graph theory, counting, sequences and induction, sets and functions again, then generating functions and a first number theory. A graph is a set of vertices and a set of edges. A tree is a connected graph with no cycle. A coloring is an assignment of labels so that neighbors differ. A matching pairs vertices under a constraint.

Counting is developed from Pascal’s triangle through combinations, permutations, multisets, combinatorial proof, a first probability, and inclusion–exclusion. Sequences are described, compared by rate of growth, and proved by induction and strong induction. Generating functions turn a sequence into a power series so that a recurrence becomes algebra.

## Why it is worth knowing

Discrete mathematics is not a vacation from calculus. It is the other half of the toolkit. An algorithm’s correctness is an induction. A network’s connectivity is a graph theorem. A hypothesis class in learning theory has a size, and the size is a count. The rate of growth of a sequence is the language of complexity: polynomial against exponential is a mathematical distinction before it is a slogan.

The joy is that the objects are finite and the theorems are sharp. Euler’s theorem on trails says exactly when a graph can be walked without repeating an edge. The four-color problem’s school form, coloring, is already deep in the planar chapter. A combinatorial proof can make a binomial identity obvious by telling a story about choosing committees. Generating functions feel like magic the first time a recurrence collapses into a closed form, and then they feel like the right language.

## What it asks of the other books

Hammack (01) overlaps the first chapter, logic and proofs, and the counting and induction. Levin is written to be readable without that book, and the curriculum still places Hammack first so that the proofs are not your first proofs. Algebra from the entry books is assumed. Calculus is not required for the combinatorics or the graphs. Power series from Calculus Volume 2 (04) make generating functions feel like old friends. On the pure and AI paths, Levin sits between Volume 2 and Volume 3 for that reason. The applied path omits Levin. The AI path keeps it, because learning theory counts and because graphs remain exact models.

Raji (12) continues the number-theory section. Grinstead and Snell (08) continue the short probability applications. Bach (29) and the counting arguments in learning theory use the combinations and the growth rates.

## A walk through the chapters

### 1. Logic and Proofs

Logic and Proofs. Mathematical statements, implications, rules of logic, proofs, and proofs about discrete structures. This is a shorter path through Hammack’s opening craft, aimed at the arguments this book needs. Reading both is a feature. The second time, the templates are yours.

### 2. Graph Theory

Graph Theory. Problems and definitions, trees, planar graphs, Euler trails and circuits, coloring, relations and graphs, and matching in bipartite graphs. A tree on n vertices has n−1 edges. A connected graph has an Euler circuit exactly when every vertex has even degree. Planarity and coloring are where the subject stops being a pile of definitions and becomes a constraint you can feel. Matching in bipartite graphs is the theorem behind assigning jobs, courses, or partners under exclusions. Relations reappear as graphs, so Hammack’s relation chapter has a picture.

### 3. Counting

Counting. Pascal’s triangle, combining outcomes, outcomes that are not disjoint, combinations and permutations, multisets, combinatorial proofs, applications to probability, and advanced counting with inclusion–exclusion. The probability section is a bridge into Grinstead and Snell, not the probability course. Inclusion–exclusion corrects a count that double-counted the overlaps. Hammack’s counting chapter and this one are two expositions of one subject. Stay with whichever voice makes the binomial coefficients feel inevitable.

### 4. Sequences

Sequences. Describing sequences, rate of growth, polynomial sequences, exponential sequences, induction, and strong induction. Rate of growth is the chapter a computer scientist will recognize, and a mathematician needs it too: the terms that dominate a sum, and the comparison of a polynomial with an exponential, are the same comparisons series tests use.

### 5. Discrete Structures Revisited

Discrete Structures Revisited. Sets and functions, briefly, as a return. If Hammack is fresh, this is a check. If it is not, this is the repair before the additional topics.

### 6. Additional Topics

Additional Topics. Generating functions, and an introduction to number theory. A generating function packages a sequence as the coefficients of a series. Operations on the series become operations on the sequence. The number theory introduction, divisibility and congruences in a first form, is the doorway into Raji, who makes the integers a whole course.
