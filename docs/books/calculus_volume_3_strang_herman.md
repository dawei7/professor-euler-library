## What this book trains

Calculus, Volume 3, the third OpenStax volume by Strang and Herman, leaves the line for the plane and then for space. Parametric curves and polar coordinates return, then vectors, the dot product, the cross product, lines, planes and quadric surfaces. Vector-valued functions describe motion, arc length and curvature. Functions of several variables have partial derivatives, a tangent plane, a chain rule, a gradient, maxima and minima, and Lagrange multipliers.

Multiple integrals compute area, volume, mass and moments in two and three variables, in rectangular, polar, cylindrical and spherical coordinates, with a change of variables for the general case. Vector calculus is the summit: vector fields, line integrals, conservative fields, Green’s theorem, divergence and curl, surface integrals, Stokes’ theorem and the divergence theorem. A final chapter treats second-order differential equations, homogeneous and nonhomogeneous, with applications and series solutions.

## Why it is worth knowing

The derivative in several variables is a linear map. The tangent plane is its graph. The matrix of partial derivatives is its table. That single fact is why linear algebra and calculus are one subject, and why Axler’s operators feel necessary after this book rather than ornamental. The gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent. Lagrange multipliers say how a constraint changes the gradient. Optimization in Suh’s book is this geometry with inequalities and convexity added.

Green, Stokes and the divergence theorem are one theorem in three dresses: the integral of a derivative over a region equals the integral of the original thing over the boundary. The fundamental theorem of Calculus Volume 1 is the one-dimensional case. Seeing the pattern is one of the great pleasures of the undergraduate sequence. Physics reads them as conservation. Mathematics reads them as Stokes’ theorem waiting for a manifold, which this book does not require. The divergence and curl are the two pieces of information a vector field’s derivative carries in three dimensions.

## What it asks of the other books

Calculus Volumes 1 and 2 (02, 04), including techniques of integration, series, parametric curves and polar coordinates. The opening chapter of Volume 3 repeats the parametric and polar material so the repetition can repair a gap. Vectors and the dot product were previewed at the end of Stitz–Zeager (S.04). Hefferon (03) makes the tangent-plane chapter and the change-of-variables Jacobian into linear algebra you already speak. The curriculum’s pure and AI paths place Hefferon before this volume for that reason. The cross product and the vector calculus are new here even if Hefferon is finished. Linear maps in Hefferon do not replace Stokes’ theorem.

Lebl’s second analysis volume (15) rebuilds several variables with proofs. Speck’s partial differential equations (21) use the divergence theorem and the language of fields. The second-order equations at the end are a bridge into Lebl’s differential equations (13), which treats them as a vector space of solutions.

## A walk through the chapters

### 1. Parametric Equations and Polar Coordinates reprises Volume 2

Parametric Equations and Polar Coordinates reprises Volume 2: parametric equations, their calculus, polar coordinates, area and arc length, and the conic sections. Start Volume 3 here if those were shaky. Move on if they were not.

### 2. Vectors in Space

Vectors in Space. Vectors in the plane and in three dimensions, the dot product, the cross product, equations of lines and planes, quadric surfaces, and cylindrical and spherical coordinates. The dot product detects a right angle and computes a projection. The cross product produces a vector perpendicular to two others, with length equal to the area of the parallelogram they span. A plane is a point and a normal vector. Quadric surfaces are the three-dimensional conics: ellipsoids, paraboloids, hyperboloids. Cylindrical and spherical coordinates are the coordinate systems the triple integrals will prefer when the region is round.

### 3. Vector-Valued Functions

Vector-Valued Functions. Space curves, their calculus, arc length and curvature, and motion in space. Velocity is the derivative of position. Acceleration is the derivative of velocity. Curvature measures how fast the tangent turns. This is Corral’s circular motion, freed from the circle.

### 4. Differentiation of Functions of Several Variables

Differentiation of Functions of Several Variables. Functions of several variables, limits and continuity, partial derivatives, tangent planes and linear approximations, the chain rule, directional derivatives and the gradient, maxima and minima, and Lagrange multipliers. A partial derivative holds the other variables still. The tangent plane uses them all at once. Clairaut’s theorem, equality of mixed partials under a continuity hypothesis, is the fact that makes the Hessian a serious matrix. Lagrange multipliers are the condition that the gradient of the objective is parallel to the gradient of the constraint. The constraint’s gradient is the direction you are not allowed to move.

### 5. Multiple Integration

Multiple Integration. Double integrals over rectangles and over general regions, double integrals in polar coordinates, triple integrals, cylindrical and spherical coordinates, centers of mass and moments of inertia, and change of variables. The Jacobian determinant is the local volume scaling of the coordinate change. It is a determinant in Hefferon’s sense, appearing here as the price of a substitution in several variables. Fubini’s theorem, integrating one variable at a time, is the working method. Measure theory will later say which functions deserve it.

### 6. Vector Calculus

Vector Calculus. Vector fields, line integrals, conservative vector fields, Green’s theorem, divergence and curl, surface integrals, Stokes’ theorem, and the divergence theorem. A field is conservative when it is a gradient, and then a line integral depends only on the endpoints. Green’s theorem converts a line integral around a plane region into a double integral. Stokes’ theorem does the same for a surface and its boundary curve. The divergence theorem converts a flux through a closed surface into a triple integral of the divergence. Learning to recognize which theorem fits which picture is the skill.

### 7. Second-Order Differential Equations

Second-Order Differential Equations. Homogeneous and nonhomogeneous linear equations, applications, and series solutions. The homogeneous equation’s solutions form a vector space of dimension two. A nonhomogeneous equation needs one particular solution plus that space, the same shape as Hefferon’s general = particular + homogeneous. Series solutions are power series from Volume 2, used because not every coefficient function allows elementary solutions. Lebl’s differential equations book is the full course this chapter opens.
