# 21 Introduction to Partial Differential Equations

*Jared Speck*

## What this book trains

Jared Speck’s Introduction to Partial Differential Equations is the Fall 2011 MIT 18.152 notes. The bundle is the class meetings, a bonus problem, a final, eleven homework sets and a midterm. A partial differential equation is an equation for an unknown function of several variables in which partial derivatives appear. The course studies three model equations in detail and then the ideas that organize them: the heat equation, Laplace’s and Poisson’s equations, and the wave equation.

Later meetings classify second-order equations, bring in the Fourier transform, Schrödinger’s equation, Lagrangian field theories, and transport equations including Burgers’ equation. The notes are a theorem-and-estimate course. Uniqueness, maximum principles, fundamental solutions, Green functions, energy estimates and representation formulas are the tools, not a list of tricks for separation of variables. Separation of variables was Lebl’s differential-equations introduction. This is the next depth.

## Why it is worth knowing

The heat equation spreads a temperature. Laplace’s equation describes a steady state. The wave equation carries a signal at finite speed. Seeing the three side by side is the education, because a method that is natural for one is wrong for another. A maximum principle for the heat equation says the hottest point of a solution on a bounded region in space-time is on the boundary of the region in the right sense. That single fact gives uniqueness for the Dirichlet problem. Energy estimates for the wave equation play the corresponding role: an energy that does not grow controls the solution.

Kirchhoff’s formula and the method of spherical means explain why waves in three space dimensions behave differently from waves on a line. Huygens’ principle, sharp propagation, is a geometric fact about odd dimensions. Burgers’ equation, at the end, is the first nonlinear shock: a smooth velocity profile can steepen until the derivative blows up. The joy of the course is that these are proved, not simulated.

## What it asks of the other books

Calculus Volume 3 (06) for the divergence theorem, gradients and the classical vector calculus. Lebl’s differential equations (13) for the heat, wave and Laplace equations in one space dimension and for Fourier series. Lebl II (15) for the implicit-function and differentiation theorems if you want the sharper estimates to feel familiar. Measure and functional analysis help the Fourier-transform meetings and are the curriculum’s recommended predecessors on the applied path. The early meetings on the heat equation can be read when multivariable calculus and the one-dimensional PDE chapter are solid.

The numerical PDE course (23) and the Delft companion (C.03) discretize equations of this kind. Speck does not replace them, and they do not replace the uniqueness proofs.

## A walk through the chapters

### Meetings 1–5

Class meeting 1 asks what a PDE is. Meetings 2 through 5 are the diffusion equation: the heat equation, uniqueness for a Dirichlet problem, the weak maximum principle, and the fundamental solution on Euclidean space. The fundamental solution is the response to a point source, and general solutions of the inhomogeneous equation are built from it by convolution.

### Meetings 6–9

Meetings 6 through 9 turn to Laplace’s equation and Poisson’s equation. The fundamental solution of the Laplacian, Green functions on a domain, Poisson’s formula, Harnack’s inequality and Liouville’s theorem. A Green function adjusts the fundamental solution so that a boundary condition is met. Harnack controls the values of a positive harmonic function inside a region by its values nearby. Liouville says a bounded harmonic function on all of space is constant, the same surname as the complex-analysis theorem and a close relative.

### Meetings 10–14

Meetings 10 through 12, and the notes that continue through meeting 14, are the wave equation. The method of spherical means, Kirchhoff’s formula, a look at Minkowski geometry, and geometric energy estimates built from an energy-momentum tensor and compatible currents. Finite propagation speed is the theorem you should be able to point at when you leave these meetings.

### Meetings 15–24

Meeting 15 classifies second-order equations and reviews the three models. Meetings 16 through 18 introduce the Fourier transform on Euclidean space, after recalling Fourier series of periodic functions. Meetings 19 and 20 are Schrödinger’s equation, the fundamental linear PDE of a single quantum particle. Meetings 21 through 23 realize many of these equations as Euler–Lagrange equations of a Lagrangian. Meeting 24 introduces transport equations and Burgers’ equation, the nonlinear conservation law in which a wave can break. A bonus problem, the final examination, eleven homework sets and the midterm close the bundle.
