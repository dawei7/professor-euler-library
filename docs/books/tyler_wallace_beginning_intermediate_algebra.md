## What this book trains

Beginning and Intermediate Algebra, by Tyler Wallace, is the grammar course. An equation is a claim that two expressions name the same number, and a legal step is an operation you could undo. The book stays with that idea from one-step equations through quadratics, rational expressions and the first functions.

You learn to keep the domain in sight. A denominator forbids its own roots. A square root hides a sign. An absolute value splits into cases. A logarithm, when it finally appears, will demand a positive argument. Factoring stops being a trick and becomes the statement that a product is zero only when a factor is zero, so a polynomial knows something about its roots.

A function arrives late, on purpose, after the expressions are fluent. Function notation, operations, inverses, exponentials, logarithms and a first look at sine and cosine are the objects calculus will stop treating as recipes and start treating as things with graphs, limits and derivatives.

## Why it is worth knowing

Every later page in the library is written in this grammar. A derivative is a limit of a difference quotient, and the quotient is algebra. A row reduction is arithmetic with a purpose. A probability calculation is a fraction that still has to obey the rules of fractions. When a later book feels slippery, the slip is often a domain, a sign, or a factoring step that this book already named.

There is a quiet joy in watching a messy expression become a simple one for a reason. Completing the square turns a quadratic into a picture of a parabola. Rational exponents make roots and powers the same operation. Complex numbers appear here as the numbers you need when a square is negative, long before they become the scalars of a complex vector space.

## What it asks of the other books

This is the entry book. It asks for ordinary arithmetic with integers and fractions, which it also reviews in the opening chapter. Nothing else in the curriculum is assumed.

It is the book S.04 reweaves, the book geometry uses when a line becomes an equation, and the book trigonometry uses when an identity is just algebra in sine and cosine. Calculus Volumes 1, 2 and 3 assume its functions. Hefferon’s linear algebra assumes you can handle the arithmetic of a system without fear.

## A walk through the chapters

### 1. Pre-Algebra

Pre-Algebra rebuilds the floor: integers, fractions, the order of operations, and the properties of algebra that later steps silently use. Distributivity is the reason you may expand a product. Commutativity is the reason you may reorder a sum. Knowing which property you used is the difference between a calculation and a habit.

### 2. Solving Linear Equations

Solving Linear Equations is the first sustained practice of reversible steps. One-step equations, two-step equations, general linear equations, equations with fractions, formulas, and absolute value equations. Variation, and the applications to number, geometry, age and distance, are where the same algebra describes a situation instead of a drill. Rearranging a formula is the skill a science course keeps borrowing.

### 3. Graphing

Graphing puts the algebra on the plane. Points and lines, slope, slope-intercept form, point-slope form, and the tests for parallel and perpendicular lines. A line is now both a picture and an equation. The distance you will later call Pythagoras is already hiding in the coordinate plane.

### 4. Inequalities

Inequalities teach you that a solution can be a set rather than a single number. Solve and graph, compound inequalities, absolute value inequalities. The graph of an inequality is a region. That idea returns as a feasible set in optimization, and as an interval in analysis.

### 5. Systems of Equations

Systems of Equations asks two claims to be true at once. Graphing, substitution, addition and elimination, then three variables. The applications to value problems and mixture problems are linear combinations in street clothes. Hefferon will take this chapter and turn it into the theory of a linear system.

### 6. Polynomials

Polynomials are the expressions built from sums and products of a variable. Exponent properties, negative exponents, scientific notation, multiplying, special products, and division. The division algorithm for polynomials is a preview of the division algorithm for integers in number theory.

### 7. Factoring

Factoring takes those products apart. Greatest common factor, grouping, trinomials with leading coefficient 1 and with a general leading coefficient, special products, a strategy for choosing a method, and solving by factoring. This is the chapter that makes the zero-product principle a tool.

### 8. Rational Expressions

Rational Expressions are quotients of polynomials. Reduce, multiply and divide, the least common denominator, add and subtract, complex fractions, proportions, solving rational equations, and dimensional analysis. Every cancelled factor is a domain condition you are not allowed to forget. Partial fractions in Calculus Volume 2 are this chapter grown up.

### 9. Radicals

Radicals extend the arithmetic to roots. Square roots, higher roots, adding, multiplying and dividing, rationalizing denominators, rational exponents, mixed index, and complex numbers. A rational exponent is a power and a root wearing one symbol. Complex numbers enter because the square root of a negative number is a legitimate object, not a dead end.

### 10. Quadratics

Quadratics solves what factoring cannot always see. Solving with radicals and exponents, completing the square, the quadratic formula, building a quadratic from its roots, equations that are quadratic in form, and the applications to rectangles, teamwork, revenue and distance. The graphs of quadratics are parabolas, the first nonlinear shape you can predict from the algebra.

### 11. Functions

Functions is the summit of the book and the doorway out of it. Function notation, operations on functions, inverse functions, exponential functions, logarithmic functions, compound interest, and a first meeting with trigonometric functions and their inverses. An inverse exists when the function is one-to-one. An exponential and a logarithm undo each other. Compound interest is the exponential you can feel. The trigonometric sections are a greeting; Corral and Stitz–Zeager are the courses.
