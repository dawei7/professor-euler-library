*CK-12 Foundation*

## What this book trains

CK-12 Geometry is the first place a proof is about a picture you can still be wrong about. Points, lines, angles and polygons get definitions precise enough to argue from. Congruence says when two figures are the same shape in different clothes. Similarity says when they are the same shape at a different scale. Circles carry inscribed angles. Area and volume turn those theorems into measurement.

The coordinate plane is not a separate subject here. Parallel and perpendicular lines, the distance formula, and the equation of a circle put the Euclidean statements back into algebra. A rigid transformation, translation, reflection, rotation and their composition, is a function of the plane. Symmetry and tessellation are what those functions look like when they leave a pattern unchanged.

Right-triangle trigonometry appears in one chapter, as a consequence of similar triangles and Pythagoras, including a short extension to the laws of sines and cosines. Corral is the book that then lives in that material. This book’s job is to make the triangle theorems things you can prove.

## Why it is worth knowing

Geometry trains a kind of honesty that algebra drills do not. A diagram suggests a fact. The fact still needs the hypothesis that makes it true. The converse of Pythagoras is a different theorem from Pythagoras. An inscribed angle is half the arc it sees, and the picture will tempt you to halve the wrong thing.

The usefulness is everywhere a later book draws. The distance formula is the Euclidean norm. Similar triangles are why a slope is a ratio. Congruence criteria are the first classification theorems: a short list of measurements determines the whole figure. Volume formulas are integrals you will later derive rather than memorize. Transformations are the intuitive form of a linear isometry, long before Axler defines one.

## What it asks of the other books

Wallace (S.01) supplies the algebra of lines, formulas and proportions. The geometry can begin as soon as equations and the coordinate plane are comfortable. You do not need trigonometry first. This book introduces the right-triangle ratios itself.

Corral (S.03) expects the triangle theorems, congruence, similarity and Pythagoras. Stitz–Zeager (S.04) expects the coordinate plane and the conic intuition that circles begin here. Calculus uses the area and volume pictures as motivation, then replaces the memorized formulas with integrals.

## A walk through the chapters

### 1. Basics of Geometry

Basics of Geometry fixes the language: points, lines and planes, segments and distance, angles and their measure, midpoints and bisectors, angle pairs, and the classification of polygons. Almost every later proof quotes a definition from this chapter.

### 2. Reasoning and Proof

Reasoning and Proof is the method. Inductive reasoning notices a pattern. A conditional statement gives the pattern a hypothesis and a conclusion. Deductive reasoning, algebraic and congruence properties, and proofs about angle pairs and segments are where the pattern has to survive being written down. This is a school form of the craft Hammack will make a whole book.

### 3. Parallel and Perpendicular Lines

Parallel and Perpendicular Lines. Corresponding angles, the properties that force lines to be parallel, perpendicular lines, and the coordinate forms of both. The distance formula in this chapter is Pythagoras wearing coordinates. A slope is a ratio of rise to run, so parallel lines share a slope and perpendicular lines have slopes that multiply to −1, once neither line is vertical.

### 4. Triangles and Congruence

Triangles and Congruence. The angle sum, congruent figures, and the criteria SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS and HL. Isosceles and equilateral triangles are the first place a symmetry of the figure forces a numerical coincidence. The criteria matter because they say which measurements are enough. Measuring everything is not a proof.

### 5. Relationships with Triangles

Relationships with Triangles goes inside the triangle: midsegments, perpendicular bisectors, angle bisectors, medians and altitudes, and the inequalities that say a longer side faces a larger angle. The extension on indirect proof is a first proof by contradiction, aimed at a geometric claim.

### 6. Polygons and Quadrilaterals

Polygons and Quadrilaterals. Interior angles of a polygon, then the family of quadrilaterals: parallelograms, the tests that prove a quadrilateral is one, rectangles, rhombuses, squares, trapezoids and kites. Classification is the mathematical pleasure here. Each extra hypothesis removes a degree of freedom and adds a theorem.

### 7. Similarity

Similarity. Ratios and proportions, similar polygons, the criteria AA, SSS and SAS for similarity, proportionality relationships such as the intercept theorems, and similarity transformations. The extension on self-similarity is a glance at a shape that contains copies of itself. Similarity is the geometric reason a trigonometric ratio depends on the angle and not on the size of the triangle.

### 8. Right Triangle Trigonometry

Right Triangle Trigonometry earns Pythagoras and its converse, the geometric mean relationships in similar right triangles, the special right triangles, then tangent, sine and cosine and their inverses. The extension states the law of sines and the law of cosines. Corral will prove and use those laws as a full subject. Here they are the promise that a triangle which is not right-angled still yields its sides.

### 9. Circles

Circles. Tangent lines, arcs, chords, inscribed angles, the angles formed by chords, secants and tangents, and the corresponding segment lengths. The extension writes the equation of a circle. An inscribed angle is the first theorem that feels like a magic trick and then becomes obvious from the picture’s symmetries.

### 10. Perimeter and Area, then Surface Area and Volume

Perimeter and Area, then Surface Area and Volume, turn the theorems into measurement: triangles, parallelograms, trapezoids, rhombi, kites, similar polygons, circumference and arc length, sectors, regular polygons, prisms, cylinders, pyramids, cones, spheres, and what happens to area and volume under similarity. A linear scale factor of k multiplies areas by k² and volumes by k³. Calculus will derive the harder formulas. The scaling law is already the deep fact.

### 11. Rigid Transformations

Rigid Transformations closes the book with symmetry, translations and vectors, reflections, rotations, composition, and tessellations. A vector appears as a translation. Composition of transformations is function composition on the plane. A tessellation is a symmetry group you can see.
