# CS.07 Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback

License: CC BY 4.0 · Source: <https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08073> · 34 pages, 0 exercises in the prepared bundle

*Yuntao Bai, Saurav Kadavath, Sandipan Kundu, Amanda Askell and others (Anthropic)*

## What this book trains

Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback is a research paper, not a textbook. It asks whether a language-model assistant can be trained to be harmless without any human label marking an output as harmful. The oversight for harmlessness is a short list of principles in natural language, the constitution, plus a few examples for few-shot prompting.

The method has two stages. In the supervised stage a helpful assistant answers prompts written to provoke harmful replies, critiques its answer against a principle drawn at random, and revises it. A pretrained model is finetuned on the revisions. In the reinforcement-learning stage that model gives two answers to each prompt, a feedback model picks the less harmful one as a multiple-choice question, and a preference model trained on those choices and on human helpfulness comparisons becomes the reward. The paper calls this RL from AI Feedback, RLAIF.

## Why it is worth knowing

In reinforcement learning from human feedback the reward is already a model, fitted to tens of thousands of human comparisons. The paper asks how much of that data must come from people. For harmlessness, none: a language model reading sixteen principles, chosen ad hoc, the paper says, supplies the labels. It also aims at a failure of the authors’ earlier assistant, which turned evasive because crowdworkers had rewarded evasion. The goal is an assistant that declines a harmful request and explains its objection.

The claims rest on Elo scores from crowdworkers, who were told to prefer the less evasive of two equally harmless answers, on preference-model scores, and on 438 binary comparison questions. The main RL runs use 52-billion-parameter models. The RL-CAI models are reported less harmful at a given helpfulness than models trained with human feedback, and virtually never evasive. Overtrained, they can turn harsh or repeat boilerplate. Labels written after a chain of thought come out near 0 or 1, and clamping them to 40–60 percent kept the policy from extreme responses.

## What it asks of the other books

The paper takes pretrained language models, few-shot prompts, chains of thought and instruction tuning for granted; Paaß and Giesselbach (CS.06) supply them. Sutton and Barto (CS.05) supply a policy, a reward signal and the idea of training a policy against a learned reward. The paper does not restate its RL algorithm; it reuses the settings of its authors’ earlier RLHF work. Prince (28) covers transformers and finetuning, and probability from 08 or CS.01 covers a probability used as a training target, and calibration.

On the Computer Science & AI path the paper stands in the last stage, Language models and alignment, after CS.06 and beside Direct Preference Optimization (CS.08). The two are read as a pair. Both start from RLHF. This paper replaces the human harmlessness labels by AI feedback guided by principles. DPO removes the separate reward model and the reinforcement-learning loop. Together they close the Computer science & AI shelf. The license recorded in the catalog is CC BY 4.0.

## A walk through the chapters

The paper prints no exercises, so its bundle carries the PDF and its contents and no exercise frames, and there is no derivation to rework. A careful reader can put two responses from Appendix D into the multiple-choice format of Section 4.1 with a principle from Appendix C, and choose on their own sheet.

### Sections 1–2

Section 1 gives the motivations and introduces the helpful RLHF models, trained on human helpfulness data alone, and the HH RLHF models kept for comparison. Section 2 asks whether language models can pick the more helpful, honest and harmless of two responses. Chain of thought helps, and the trend suggests that larger models will be competitive with preference models trained on human feedback.

### Sections 3–4

Section 3 is the supervised stage: 182,831 red-team prompts, most of them generated by a model, four rounds of critique and revision each, and helpfulness samples mixed in so that SL-CAI stays helpful. Harmlessness scores rise with each revision. Section 3.5 finds direct revision about as good for large models and the critiques often overstated, but keeps them for transparency. Section 4 is the RL stage: soft targets from the probabilities of options (A) and (B), then hard and clamped labels, ensembling over principles, evasiveness, and an absolute harmfulness score from 0 to 4.

### Sections 5–7

Related work places the paper beside RLHF for language models, LaMDA, InstructGPT and Sparrow, self-critique and red teaming. The discussion notes that human supervision remains in the helpfulness labels, names future directions, and warns that cheaper steering also makes harmful systems easier to train. Section 7 states who did what.

### Appendices A–E

Appendix A shows four successive critiques and revisions of one response. Appendix B asks models to tell harmful from ethical behavior and to sort harmful conversations into nine categories. Appendix C lists the sixteen critique and revision requests for SL-CAI and the sixteen principles for RL-CAI. Appendix D compares HH RLHF with RL-CAI using chain of thought on PALMS, LaMDA and InstructGPT prompts. Appendix E prints the few-shot and chain-of-thought prompts.
