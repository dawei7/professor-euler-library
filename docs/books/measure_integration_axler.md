## What this book trains

Sheldon Axler’s Measure, Integration & Real Analysis rebuilds the integral so that limits and integrals can trade places under hypotheses you can check. The Riemann integral is reviewed and then shown to be not enough: the pointwise limit of Riemann integrable functions need not be Riemann integrable. Outer measure on the line, measurable spaces and functions, measures, Lebesgue measure, and convergence of measurable functions repair the foundation. The integral with respect to a general measure, and the theorems that pass a limit inside an integral, are the working core.

The book does not stop at the line. Differentiation of integrals and the Hardy–Littlewood maximal function. Product measures, iterated integrals, and Lebesgue integration on ℝⁿ. Banach spaces, Lᵖ spaces and duality. Hilbert spaces. Real and complex measures, total variation and decomposition theorems. Linear maps on Hilbert space, the spectrum, compact operators and the spectral theorem for compact operators. Fourier series, the Poisson integral, the Fourier transform. A final chapter on probability measures, so a random variable is a measurable function and an expectation is an integral.

## Why it is worth knowing

The dominated convergence theorem and the monotone convergence theorem are the permissions numerical analysts and probabilists keep borrowing. If a sequence of functions is dominated by one integrable function, the limit of the integrals is the integral of the limit. That is the sentence Riemann theory cannot always say. Lᵖ spaces are the functions whose p-th power is integrable, and they are Banach spaces. L² is a Hilbert space. The geometry of Axler’s finite-dimensional inner products, orthogonality and orthonormal bases, returns here for functions.

Fourier analysis is the reward. A function on the circle has Fourier coefficients, the inner products against the exponentials. The question of when the series returns the function is a theorem in Lᵖ, not a hope. The Fourier transform does the analogous job on the line. Probability measures at the end are why Polyanskiy’s course can start from this book: the axioms of probability are the axioms of a measure that assigns 1 to the whole space. The spectral theorem for compact operators is the infinite-dimensional heir of the finite-dimensional spectral theorem, under a hypothesis, compactness, that makes the eigenvalues behave.

## What it asks of the other books

Lebl I (10) for the real numbers, sequences, uniform convergence and the Riemann integral. Axler’s Linear Algebra Done Right (07) for inner products, orthogonality, adjoints and the finite-dimensional spectral theorem. The Banach and Hilbert chapters of this book review metric spaces, normed spaces and inner products, so a reader whose linear algebra is Hefferon-strong and whose analysis is Lebl-strong can repair in place. Lebl II (15) helps the several-variable intuition and Fubini’s classical form. Lebesgue’s Fubini is proved here.

Rodríguez (20) overlaps the Banach-space theorems and goes further into the named theorems of functional analysis as a lecture course. Polyanskiy (25) takes the probability chapter as a foundation and should be read after this book. The pure and applied paths both place measure before functional analysis. Complex analysis (18) is independent and may be read on either side.

## A walk through the chapters

### 1. Riemann Integration

Riemann Integration. A review of the Riemann integral, and the reasons it is not good enough. The counterexamples are the point of the chapter. They motivate every definition that follows.

### 2. Measures

Measures. Outer measure on ℝ, measurable spaces and functions, measures and their properties, Lebesgue measure, and convergence of measurable functions. An outer measure is allowed to be only countably subadditive. The measurable sets are the ones that split every test set additively, the Carathéodory condition. Lebesgue measure on those sets extends length. A function is measurable when preimages of reasonable sets are measurable, the same preimage idea as continuity, for a different family of sets. Convergence in measure and almost-everywhere convergence are the two limits the integral can see. Almost everywhere means except on a set of measure zero.

### 3. Integration

Integration. Integration with respect to a measure, and limits of integrals together with integrals of limits. Simple functions, nonnegative functions, then integrable functions. The monotone convergence theorem, Fatou’s lemma, and the dominated convergence theorem are the three tools. Learn which hypothesis each one spends.

### 4. Differentiation

Differentiation. The Hardy–Littlewood maximal function, and derivatives of integrals. The maximal function controls the local averages of a function. It is the tool that proves the Lebesgue differentiation theorem: the average of an integrable function over a shrinking ball returns the function’s value, almost everywhere. The fundamental theorem acquires a measure-theoretic form.

### 5. Product Measures

Product Measures. Products of measure spaces, iterated integrals, and Lebesgue integration on ℝⁿ. Fubini’s theorem and Tonelli’s theorem say when a multiple integral equals an iterated integral. Tonelli asks for nonnegativity. Fubini asks for integrability. Calculus Volume 3 used the conclusion. This chapter names the hypothesis.

### 6. Banach Spaces, and Lᵖ Spaces

Banach Spaces, and Lᵖ Spaces. Metric spaces, vector spaces, normed spaces, linear functionals, and consequences of Baire’s theorem. Then the inequalities that make Lᵖ a normed space, and Lᵖ as a Banach space with its dual. Hölder’s inequality is the inner-product estimate generalized. Baire’s theorem, that a complete metric space is not a countable union of nowhere dense sets, is the engine behind the uniform boundedness principle and the other named theorems Rodríguez’s lectures will dwell on. The dual of Lᵖ is Lᵠ for the conjugate exponent, under the usual restrictions. That identification is a summit of the chapter.

### 7. Hilbert Spaces

Hilbert Spaces. Inner product spaces, orthogonality, and orthonormal bases. Projection onto a closed convex set, orthonormal expansion, and the fact that a Hilbert space is determined by the size of an orthonormal basis. This is Axler’s chapter on inner products, with completeness added and with bases that may be infinite.

### 8. Real and Complex Measures, and Linear Maps on Hilbert Spaces

Real and Complex Measures, and Linear Maps on Hilbert Spaces. Total variation, decomposition theorems, adjoints and invertibility, the spectrum, compact operators, and the spectral theorem for compact operators. A signed measure is a difference of measures. The Hahn and Jordan decompositions make that precise. The spectrum of an operator is the set of scalars for which it fails to be invertible, the right generalization of the set of eigenvalues. A compact operator sends the unit ball to a set whose closure is compact. For a compact self-adjoint operator the spectral theorem returns an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, with eigenvalues tending to zero. The finite-dimensional theorem is the case in which every operator is compact.

### 9. Fourier Analysis, and Probability Measures

Fourier Analysis, and Probability Measures. Fourier series and the Poisson integral, Fourier series in Lᵖ of the circle, the Fourier transform, and probability measures. The Poisson integral solves the Dirichlet problem by convolution against the Poisson kernel, the complex-analysis and PDE fact, now an Lᵖ theorem. The Fourier transform extends the series to the line. The probability chapter specializes measure to total mass one and points to the next course.
