# 18 Guide to Cultivating Complex Analysis: Working the Complex Field

*Jiří Lebl*

## What this book trains

Jiří Lebl’s Guide to Cultivating Complex Analysis is the rigidity of holomorphic functions. A function that is complex-differentiable on an open set is analytic: it equals a power series. One derivative implies infinitely many. Cauchy’s theorem turns integrals over closed curves into statements about what is inside. The residue theorem evaluates those integrals from local data at the singularities. A holomorphic function that is not identically zero has isolated zeros. If the zeros accumulate inside the domain, the function is zero everywhere.

The book goes past a first course. The logarithm and the winding number, homology and homotopy forms of Cauchy’s theorem, Laurent series, Montel’s theorem and the Riemann mapping theorem, harmonic functions and the Dirichlet problem, infinite products and Weierstrass factorization, Runge’s theorem and Mittag-Leffler, analytic continuation, and the Schwarz reflection principle. Appendices review metric spaces and the real-analysis facts the proofs quote.

## Why it is worth knowing

Complex analysis is the place where computation and rigidity meet. A real integral that looks impossible can be a residue of a cleverly chosen complex function. Liouville’s theorem, a bounded entire function is constant, falls out of Cauchy’s estimates, and the fundamental theorem of algebra falls out of Liouville. You have seen that theorem stated in precalculus and proved from the maximum principle in Lebl II. Here it is a one-page consequence of the integral.

The Riemann mapping theorem is the geometric prize. Any simply connected planar domain that is not the whole plane is biholomorphic to the disk. The shape you see and the complex structure are almost independent: there is always a conformal map to the disk. Schwarz’s lemma controls the automorphisms of that disk, which are the Möbius transformations Hitchman used as a geometry. Harmonic functions, the real and imaginary parts of holomorphic functions, solve the steady-temperature problem Lebl’s differential equations posed on a disk.

## What it asks of the other books

Lebl I (10) and Lebl II (15) for real analysis, uniform convergence, power series, path integrals, Green’s theorem and the inverse function theorem. The book’s own appendices, Metric Spaces and Results From Basic Analysis, collect sequences of functions, Fubini, derivatives under the integral, and the derivative in several real variables. Use them as a repair. Calculus Volume 3 (06) for line integrals and Green’s theorem in the classical language. Hitchman (17) is a friendly neighbor for Möbius transformations and the disk, not a prerequisite.

Measure theory is not required. Holomorphic functions do not need the Lebesgue integral. The pure path may read this book before or after Axler’s measure book (19). Functional analysis is not required either. Montel’s theorem uses Arzelà–Ascoli, which Lebl II already proved.

## A walk through the chapters

### 1. The Complex Plane

The Complex Plane. Complex numbers, polar form and the exponential, the Riemann sphere, linear fractional transformations, and the cross ratio. The Riemann sphere is the extended plane, Hitchman’s infinity made into a sphere by stereographic projection. A linear fractional transformation is a Möbius map. The cross ratio is the invariant that tells you when four points can be mapped to four other points.

### 2. Holomorphic and Analytic Functions

Holomorphic and Analytic Functions. The Cauchy–Riemann equations, basic properties, power series, and analytic functions. Cauchy–Riemann is the statement that the real derivative, a 2-by-2 matrix, is a complex multiplication. Power series converge to holomorphic functions. The converse, every holomorphic function is analytic, is a consequence of Cauchy’s integral formula in the next chapters. It is the rigidity.

### 3. Line Integrals and Rudimentary Cauchy Theorems

Line Integrals and Rudimentary Cauchy Theorems. Line integrals, starter forms of Cauchy’s theorem, consequences, the Cauchy transform and convergence, and Schwarz’s lemma with the automorphisms of the disk. Cauchy’s integral formula recovers the function from its values on a circle. Differentiating under the integral gives the higher derivatives. Schwarz’s lemma says a holomorphic self-map of the disk that fixes the origin is a contraction in modulus, and the automorphisms are the rotations composed with a simple Möbius factor.

### 4. The Logarithm and Cauchy

The Logarithm and Cauchy. The logarithm and the winding number, homology versions of Cauchy, simply connected domains, Laurent series, and the further forms of Cauchy by homotopy, by Green’s theorem, and for piecewise-smooth boundaries. The logarithm is not single-valued on the punctured plane. The winding number counts how a closed curve wraps a point, and it is an integral. A Laurent series is the two-sided series that describes a function on an annulus, and its principal part is the singularity.

### 5. Counting Zeros and Singularities

Counting Zeros and Singularities. Zeros, isolated singularities, the residue theorem, counting zeros and poles, the open mapping theorem, and inverses of holomorphic functions. Removable singularities, poles and essential singularities are the three isolated possibilities. The residue theorem is the computational form of Cauchy. The argument principle counts zeros minus poles by a winding number. The open mapping theorem says a nonconstant holomorphic function sends open sets to open sets. An inverse exists locally where the derivative is nonzero, the complex form of the inverse function theorem.

### 6. Montel and Riemann

Montel and Riemann. Equicontinuity and Arzelà–Ascoli, Montel’s theorem, and the Riemann mapping theorem. Montel says a locally bounded family of holomorphic functions is a normal family: every sequence has a subsequence converging uniformly on compact subsets. That compactness is the engine of the Riemann mapping theorem.

### 7. Harmonic Functions

Harmonic Functions. Harmonic functions, the Dirichlet problem in a disk and applications, extending harmonic functions, and subharmonic functions. A harmonic function satisfies Laplace’s equation. On a disk the Poisson kernel from the differential-equations course reappears as the solution operator. Subharmonic functions are the inequality version, useful for maximum principles.

### 8. Weierstrass Factorization, Rational Approximation, and Analytic Continuation

Weierstrass Factorization, Rational Approximation, and Analytic Continuation. Infinite products, the Weierstrass product for entire functions with prescribed zeros, polynomial approximation, Runge’s theorem, the polynomial hull, Mittag-Leffler’s theorem on prescribed principal parts, the Schwarz reflection principle, and continuation along paths. Weierstrass builds a function with zeros where you asked. Mittag-Leffler builds one with poles where you asked. Runge approximates a holomorphic function on a compact set by rational functions, and by polynomials when the complement is connected. Analytic continuation is the reason an identity on a small disk can spread to every point a path can reach without hitting a singularity. The metric-space and basic-analysis appendices close the book.
