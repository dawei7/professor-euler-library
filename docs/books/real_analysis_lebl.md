## What this book trains

Jiří Lebl’s Basic Analysis I rebuilds the calculus of one variable with the hypotheses restored. The real numbers are a complete ordered field. Completeness is the axiom that makes limits exist: every Cauchy sequence converges, and every nonempty set that is bounded above has a least upper bound. Sequences, series, continuity, the derivative, the Riemann integral, sequences of functions, and a first chapter of metric spaces.

The famous named theorems become theorems. The extreme value theorem, the intermediate value theorem, the mean value theorem, Taylor’s theorem, the inverse function theorem, and the fundamental theorem of calculus. Uniform continuity and uniform convergence are the new distinctions. A pointwise limit of continuous functions need not be continuous. A uniform limit is. That single gap is why analysis has to be more careful than calculus.

## Why it is worth knowing

Numerical analysis says “the error goes to zero”. This book is where that sentence acquires a proof. Compactness, met here at the end in metric spaces and earlier through the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem, is the hypothesis that forces a continuous function on a closed bounded interval to attain its maximum. Without it, a function can be continuous and unbounded on a set that looks innocent.

The pleasure is seeing the calculus you can already do become inevitable. The logarithm and the exponential are constructed from the integral, so their properties are theorems rather than button labels. Picard’s theorem, appearing with sequences of functions and again with the fixed point theorem, is the existence proof for solutions of ordinary differential equations. It is the moment analysis pays back the differential-equations course. Interchange of limits is the theme that measure theory will later settle for integrals. Here you meet the theme with series and with uniform convergence.

## What it asks of the other books

Calculus Volumes 1 and 2 (02, 04), so the theorems have examples. Hammack (01) for quantifiers, sets, induction and the informal ε–δ meeting in his calculus chapter. This book is the course that chapter promised. The entry algebra and the completeness of your inequality practice matter more than trigonometry.

Volume II (15) continues into several variables and a harder integral. Axler’s measure book (19) replaces the Riemann integral when the interchange theorems need a bigger class of functions. Badzioch’s topology (16) takes the open sets of the metric-space chapter and drops the metric. Read this volume before those three.

## A walk through the chapters

### 1. Introduction

Introduction. A short orientation, about analysis, and a reminder of basic set theory. If Hammack is fresh, the set theory is a check of notation. The orientation is worth reading slowly: it says what kind of argument the book accepts.

### 2. Real Numbers

Real Numbers. Basic properties, the set of real numbers, absolute value and bounded functions, intervals and the size of ℝ, and decimal representations. The size of the reals reconnects with Hammack’s uncountability. Decimal expansions are a construction, and they are not unique for terminating decimals. Completeness is the property that separates the reals from the rationals. The rationals have gaps. The reals do not.

### 3. Sequences and Series

Sequences and Series. Limits of sequences, the facts about those limits, limit superior and limit inferior, the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem, Cauchy sequences, and two passes through series. Limit superior is the tool for sequences that do not settle. Bolzano–Weierstrass says a bounded sequence in the reals has a convergent subsequence. A Cauchy sequence is one whose terms eventually cluster, and completeness says clustering is enough for convergence. The series tests of Calculus Volume 2 return as proved statements.

### 4. Continuous Functions

Continuous Functions. Limits of functions, continuity, the extreme and intermediate value theorems, uniform continuity, limits at infinity, and monotone functions. Uniform continuity is stronger than continuity at each point, and on a closed bounded interval the two agree. That equivalence is compactness in an early form. A monotone function has very controlled discontinuities. It can jump, and the jumps are countable.

### 5. The Derivative

The Derivative. The definition, the mean value theorem, Taylor’s theorem, and the inverse function theorem. Taylor’s theorem with a remainder is the honest form of the polynomial approximation from Volume 2. The inverse function theorem in one variable says a continuously differentiable function with nonzero derivative is locally invertible, and the derivative of the inverse is the reciprocal. Several variables will need a matrix inverse in that sentence. Volume II writes it.

### 6. The Riemann Integral

The Riemann Integral. The definition, properties, the fundamental theorem, the construction of the logarithm and the exponential, and improper integrals. The Riemann integral is a limit of sums over partitions. It integrates continuous functions, and some discontinuous ones, and it fails on enough interesting functions that Volume II and the measure book have work to do. Building the logarithm as an integral of 1/t makes ln a defined object.

### 7. Sequences of Functions

Sequences of Functions. Pointwise and uniform convergence, interchange of limits, and Picard’s theorem. This is the chapter calculus never quite has. You may not always pass a limit inside an integral or a derivative. Uniform convergence is a sufficient condition you can check. Picard’s iteration builds a solution of a differential equation as a limit of functions. The metric-space chapter will explain the contraction that makes the iteration converge.

### 8. Metric Spaces

Metric Spaces. The definition, open and closed sets, sequences and convergence, completeness and compactness, continuous functions, and the fixed point theorem with Picard again. A metric is a distance. An open set is a union of balls. Compactness is the property that every open cover has a finite subcover, and in metric spaces it agrees with sequential compactness. The fixed point theorem says a contraction of a complete metric space has a unique fixed point. Picard’s theorem is that abstract fact applied to a space of functions. Badzioch begins near here and then lets the metric go.
