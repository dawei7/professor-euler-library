## What this book trains

Wissam Raji’s Elementary Number Theory studies the integers as a world with its own theorems. Divisibility, the division algorithm, the Euclidean algorithm, primes and unique factorization, linear Diophantine equations, congruences, the Chinese remainder theorem, Euler’s totient, the theorems of Fermat, Euler and Wilson, multiplicative functions, the Möbius inversion formula, primitive roots, and the law of quadratic reciprocity.

Later chapters leave the elementary core without abandoning it. Continued fractions and how well they approximate irrationals. A first look at analytic number theory through Chebyshev’s functions and the road toward the prime number theorem. Additional topics: cryptography, elliptic curves, and the Riemann zeta function.

## Why it is worth knowing

The integers are the first infinite structure anyone meets, and they are not simple. Unique factorization is a theorem, not a habit, and it fails in nearby rings, which is why algebra has to be careful. Quadratic reciprocity is a shock the first time: whether a prime is a square modulo another prime is related to the swapped question, with a sign you can compute. It is one of the great identities of elementary mathematics, and it is proved in this book.

The usefulness is practical and conceptual. The Euclidean algorithm computes a greatest common divisor in a number of steps Lamé’s theorem bounds. The Chinese remainder theorem splits a congruence into a system of smaller ones, which is both a computational trick and the structural fact that a product of rings can be a ring of tuples. Cryptography in the additional topics uses exactly these congruences. The zeta function is the doorway from this course to the analytic theory of primes. The book walks up to that door and tells you what is on it.

## What it asks of the other books

Hammack (01) for induction, contradiction, and the fundamental theorem of arithmetic in a first form. This book proves that theorem again as part of a fuller story. Levin’s short number-theory section (05) is a preview. Algebra from Wallace (S.01) for the arithmetic. Judson (11) supplies the group language that makes the multiplicative group modulo n a group, Euler’s theorem a corollary of Lagrange, and primitive roots a statement about cyclic groups. You can read Raji before Judson. The theorems still hold. They become one idea afterwards.

Analysis is not required for the elementary chapters. The analytic chapter and the zeta function want the series from Calculus Volume 2 (04) and, for comfort, Lebl I (10). Elliptic curves are a glimpse, not a second course.

## A walk through the chapters

### Chapter 1. Introduction

The front matter is the title, contents and licensing. Chapter 1, Introduction, is already mathematics: algebraic operations with integers, the well-ordering principle and induction, divisibility and the division algorithm, representations in different bases, the greatest common divisor, the Euclidean algorithm, and Lamé’s theorem on how many steps that algorithm needs. Well-ordering is the form of induction that says every nonempty set of positive integers has a least element. Many divisibility proofs are shortest in that language.

### 2. Prime Numbers

Prime Numbers. The sieve of Eratosthenes, the infinitude of primes, the fundamental theorem of arithmetic, least common multiples, linear Diophantine equations, the floor function and the symbols of growth, and theorems and conjectures about primes. Euclid’s proof that there are infinitely many primes is the model of a short, immortal argument. Unique factorization is the structural theorem. Linear Diophantine equations, ax + by = c, are solvable exactly when the gcd divides c, and the Euclidean algorithm finds a solution.

### 3. Congruences

Congruences. Residue systems, Euler’s φ-function, linear congruences, the Chinese remainder theorem, and the theorems of Fermat, Euler and Wilson. Wilson’s theorem, that (p−1)! ≡ −1 modulo a prime p, is a curiosity that becomes a characterization. Fermat and Euler are the counting theorems of the multiplicative group. The Chinese remainder theorem is the chapter’s tool: a system of congruences to coprime moduli has a unique solution modulo the product.

### 4. Number Theoretic Functions

Number Theoretic Functions. Definitions, multiplicative functions, the Möbius function and Möbius inversion, and perfect, Mersenne and Fermat numbers. A multiplicative function is determined by its values at prime powers. Möbius inversion recovers a function from its sums over divisors. It is inclusion–exclusion written for the divisor lattice. Perfect numbers and the special primes attached to them are the classical curiosities, still tied to open questions the book states as conjectures rather than as theorems.

### 5. Primitive Roots and Quadratic Residues

Primitive Roots and Quadratic Residues. The order of an integer modulo n, primitive roots, which primes have them, quadratic residues, the Legendre symbol, the law of quadratic reciprocity, and the Jacobi symbol. A primitive root is a generator of the multiplicative group. Quadratic reciprocity is the identity that makes the Legendre symbol computable. The Jacobi symbol extends the computation to composite moduli.

### 6. Continued Fractions

Continued Fractions. Notation, the technical machinery, very good approximation, an application, and a formula of Gauss with a theorem of Kuzmin and Lévy and a problem of Arnold. A continued fraction is the best way to approximate a real number by rationals, in a sense the chapter makes precise. The golden ratio and the Euclidean algorithm are the same recursion seen twice.

### 7. Introduction to Analytic Number Theory

Introduction to Analytic Number Theory. Chebyshev’s functions, and a path that gets closer to the prime number theorem. The prime number theorem says the number of primes up to x is about x / log x. This chapter builds the functions Chebyshev used to approach that statement. It is an introduction. The full proof wants more analysis than the chapter spends.

### 8. Additional Topics

Additional Topics. Cryptography, elliptic curves, and the Riemann zeta function. Cryptography uses the congruences. Elliptic curves are a newer arithmetic, hinted rather than completed. The zeta function, the series of reciprocal powers and its Euler product over primes, is why the primes and analysis are one subject. The index and glossary close the book.
