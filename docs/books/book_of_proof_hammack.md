# 01 Book of Proof

*Richard Hammack*

## What this book trains

Richard Hammack’s Book of Proof teaches the craft underneath every later university book. A definition is the whole meaning of a word. A quantifier changes the claim: “for every ε there is a δ” is a different sentence from “there is a δ for every ε”. A proof is an argument that still holds when the numbers change. A counterexample is the precise way to show which hypothesis was missing.

The objects are the ones the rest of the library speaks. Sets, including power sets, indexed unions and the number systems. Logic, up through quantifiers and negation. Counting, as a rehearsal for probability and for discrete mathematics. Functions as sets of pairs, with injective, surjective, composition and inverse. Relations, equivalence classes, and the integers modulo n. Cardinality, including countable and uncountable sets and the Cantor–Bernstein–Schröder theorem.

One chapter writes the proofs that calculus usually only uses: the definition of a limit, continuity, sequences and series. It does not replace Calculus Volume 1. It makes the language of that book readable.

## Why it is worth knowing

Without this book, analysis is a wall of quantifiers and algebra is a wall of “show that”. With it, both become games you know the rules of. Induction is a machine for the integers. Contradiction is a machine for statements whose negation is easier to attack. Existence can be constructive, you exhibit the object, or non-constructive, you prove it cannot fail to exist. Knowing which kind you have is a mathematical pleasure of its own.

The usefulness is daily. Equivalence relations are how you build modular arithmetic, quotient groups, and quotient spaces. The pigeonhole principle is a counting argument that returns in computer science and in combinatorics. Uncountability of the reals is why a list of numbers cannot exhaust the line, which is the first deep fact of analysis. Russell’s paradox is the warning that a set cannot be built from an unrestricted property, which is why later books are careful about what they call a set.

## What it asks of the other books

Algebra from Wallace (S.01), or the equivalent fluency with integers, sets of numbers and functions. Geometry and trigonometry are not required. The calculus-proofs chapter explains limits from the definition, so it can be read before Calculus Volume 1, and it makes Volume 1’s precise definition of a limit a second meeting rather than a first shock.

Levin (05) shares logic, counting, induction, sets and functions, and adds graph theory. Grinstead and Snell (08) use the counting. Lebl’s analysis (10) uses quantifiers, induction, sets and the limit language. Judson (11) uses equivalence relations. Axler’s measure book (19) uses countable and uncountable sets in earnest.

## A walk through the chapters

### 1. Sets

Sets. Introduction, Cartesian products, subsets, power sets, union, intersection and difference, complement, Venn diagrams, indexed sets, the number systems as sets, and Russell’s paradox. A power set is the set of all subsets, and it is strictly larger than the set you started with. That fact becomes Cantor’s theorem in the cardinality chapter. Indexed unions are the notation analysis uses for a countable cover.

### 2. Logic

Logic. Statements, and, or, not, conditional and biconditional statements, truth tables, logical equivalence, quantifiers, more on conditionals, translation from English, negation, and inference. Negating a quantified statement flips the quantifier and negates the inside. That one skill is half of reading an ε–δ proof. The book’s note on the difference between a formal derivation and a mathematical paragraph is worth keeping: later books write paragraphs, and the logic still has to be there.

### 3. Counting

Counting. Lists, the multiplication principle, addition and subtraction, factorials and permutations, subsets, Pascal’s triangle and the binomial theorem, inclusion–exclusion, multisets, the division and pigeonhole principles, and combinatorial proof. A combinatorial proof shows two counts are equal by describing one set in two ways. Levin’s counting chapter is a second visit to this material, aimed at discrete structures.

### 4. Direct Proof, Contrapositive Proof, and Proof by Contradiction

Direct Proof, Contrapositive Proof, and Proof by Contradiction are the three basic shapes. Theorems and definitions, direct proof, cases. Then the contrapositive, with congruence of integers as the worked example, and a section on mathematical writing. Then contradiction, including contradiction aimed at a conditional, and the advice about combining techniques. Congruence is the first algebraic structure the book proves things about, and it returns as the integers modulo n.

### 5. Proving Non-Conditional Statements

Proving Non-Conditional Statements. If-and-only-if proofs, families of equivalent statements, existence, existence and uniqueness, and constructive versus non-constructive proofs. An if-and-only-if is two implications. Uniqueness is usually a proof that any two candidates are equal. These templates are what “characterize” and “there exists a unique” mean in every later text.

### 6. Proofs Involving Sets

Proofs Involving Sets. How to prove membership, inclusion, and equality of sets, with perfect numbers as a long example. Inclusion both ways is the standard proof that two sets are equal. It is the pattern behind “the null space is the set of solutions” and behind “the image of a compact set is compact” once those words exist.

### 7. Disproof

Disproof. Counterexamples, disproving existence statements, and disproof by contradiction. A counterexample is a gift. One carefully chosen object ends a false universal claim. Hunting it trains the same instinct as hunting a hypothesis you forgot to state.

### 8. Mathematical Induction

Mathematical Induction. Ordinary induction, strong induction, proof by smallest counterexample, the fundamental theorem of arithmetic, and the Fibonacci numbers. The fundamental theorem of arithmetic, unique factorization into primes, is the prize of the chapter. Raji’s number theory builds a whole course on the integers this chapter introduces. Strong induction is the form you need when a statement calls on more than the single previous case, as Fibonacci does.

### 9. Relations

Relations. Relations and their properties, equivalence relations, equivalence classes and partitions, the integers modulo n, and relations between sets. An equivalence relation is reflexive, symmetric and transitive, and it cuts the set into classes. Modular arithmetic is the example you can compute. Judson’s groups begin from this kind of example: symmetries and integer equivalence classes.

### 10. Functions

Functions. Functions as rules and as sets of pairs, injective and surjective functions, the pigeonhole principle again, composition, inverses, image and preimage. A function has an inverse exactly when it is bijective. The image and preimage are the language of continuity in topology: a function is continuous when the preimage of an open set is open. Meeting the words here makes that definition a sentence instead of a spell.

### 11. Proofs in Calculus

Proofs in Calculus. The triangle inequality, the definition of a limit, limits that do not exist, the limit laws, continuity and derivatives, limits at infinity, sequences, and series. This is a bridge. The triangle inequality is the estimate that makes limit proofs close. Sequences and series appear here as objects with definitions, and Calculus Volume 2 is where they get their convergence tests.

### 12. Cardinality of Sets

Cardinality of Sets. Sets of equal cardinality, countable and uncountable sets, comparing cardinalities, and the Cantor–Bernstein–Schröder theorem. Two sets have the same cardinality when a bijection exists between them. The integers and the rationals are countable. The reals are not, by Cantor’s diagonal argument. The Cantor–Bernstein–Schröder theorem says that if each set injects into the other, a bijection exists, even when you cannot write it down. Analysis lives on this distinction.
