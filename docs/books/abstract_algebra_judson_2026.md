## What this book trains

Thomas W. Judson’s Abstract Algebra: Theory and Applications names the patterns that linear algebra and number theory have already been using. A group is a set with one operation, an identity, and inverses. A ring has two operations, and a field is a ring in which the nonzero elements form a group under multiplication. The integers, the invertible matrices, the symmetries of a polygon, and the scalars of a vector space are examples of structures you have met under other names.

The book is broad on purpose. Groups through the Sylow theorems and group actions. Rings, polynomials and integral domains. Lattices and Boolean algebras. Vector spaces again, then fields, finite fields and Galois theory. Two applied chapters, cryptography and algebraic coding theory, show the same theorems protecting a message and detecting an error.

## Why it is worth knowing

Abstraction here is a compression. Lagrange’s theorem says the size of a subgroup divides the size of a finite group, and suddenly Fermat’s little theorem, the structure of cyclic groups, and a fact about symmetries are one fact. The Sylow theorems say a finite group contains subgroups of prime-power order, which is how a classification begins. Galois theory connects a polynomial to the symmetries of its roots, and explains why some polynomials refuse a formula in radicals.

The usefulness is not only inside algebra. Finite fields are the alphabet of coding theory and of many cryptographic constructions. Matrix groups are the continuous symmetries of linear algebra, a bridge toward geometry. Homomorphisms are the structure-preserving maps, and the first isomorphism theorem says a homomorphic image is the original structure with the kernel collapsed. That sentence is the template for quotient spaces, quotient groups and quotient rings.

## What it asks of the other books

Hammack (01) for proofs, sets, functions and equivalence relations. The preliminaries chapter reviews proofs, sets and equivalence relations, so a gap can be patched, but the book expects you to sustain a long argument. Judson’s integers chapter uses induction and the division algorithm, which Hammack and Raji also treat. Hefferon (03) makes the vector-space and matrix-group chapters a return rather than a first meeting. Raji (12) can be read beside the integers, congruences and cryptography. Neither replaces the other. Raji computes in the integers. Judson explains which of those computations are group theory.

Lebl’s analysis is not a prerequisite. Galois theory wants polynomials and fields, which the book builds. Linear algebra done right (07) makes the vector-space chapter feel like home, and it can be read before or after. The curriculum’s pure path places Judson after Lebl I and before Raji.

## A walk through the chapters

### 1. The opening matter

The opening matter is the title, preface and acknowledgements. The mathematics begins with Preliminaries, a short note on proofs together with sets and equivalence relations, and The Integers, induction and the division algorithm. Equivalence relations are the tool that will build cosets. The division algorithm is the tool that will build the Euclidean algorithm and, later, the structure of polynomial rings.

### 2. Groups, Cyclic Groups, and Permutation Groups

Groups, Cyclic Groups, and Permutation Groups. Integer equivalence classes and symmetries, the definition, subgroups, cyclic subgroups, the multiplicative group of complex numbers, and permutations. A symmetry is a bijection that preserves structure. The symmetries of a set form a group under composition. Cyclic groups are the ones generated by a single element, and they are classified by their order. Permutations are the group of all bijections of a finite set, and every finite group sits inside one of them. That is Cayley’s idea, waiting in the later structure chapters.

### 3. Cosets and Lagrange’s Theorem

Cosets and Lagrange’s Theorem. A coset is a translate of a subgroup. The cosets partition the group, and they all have the same size, so the order of a subgroup divides the order of the group. This is the first counting theorem of the subject, and it is why a group of prime order is cyclic.

### 4. Introduction to Cryptography, and Algebraic Coding Theory

Introduction to Cryptography, and Algebraic Coding Theory. The arithmetic of congruences and of finite groups becomes a way to hide a message and a way to detect that a message was damaged. These chapters are applications with theorems, not a change of subject. They are readable when Lagrange and the integers are in hand, and they repay the effort by showing why an abstract inverse is an operation someone would want.

### 5. Isomorphisms, Normal Subgroups and Factor Groups, and Homomorphisms

Isomorphisms, Normal Subgroups and Factor Groups, and Homomorphisms. An isomorphism is a renaming. A normal subgroup is one that can be the kernel of a homomorphism, and the factor group is the image. The homomorphism theorems say how kernels, images and quotients match. Matrix Groups and Symmetry then puts the theory on invertible matrices and geometric symmetries, so the examples stay as concrete as the proofs.

### 6. The Structure of Groups, Group Actions, and the Sylow Theorems

The Structure of Groups, Group Actions, and the Sylow Theorems. A group acting on a set is a homomorphism into a group of permutations. Counting orbits is a method, not a metaphor. The Sylow theorems guarantee subgroups whose orders are the highest prime powers dividing the group’s order, and they constrain how those subgroups sit inside the group. Finite group theory after this chapter is a conversation about those constraints.

### 7. Rings, Polynomials, and Integral Domains

Rings, Polynomials, and Integral Domains. Two operations. Ideals play the role that normal subgroups played. Polynomial rings are the rings in which Galois theory will live. An integral domain is a ring in which a product is zero only when a factor is zero, the axiom Wallace used for polynomials, now isolated. Lattices and Boolean Algebras are the ordered cousins: a Boolean algebra is the algebra of sets under union, intersection and complement, and of logical propositions under the matching operations.

### 8. Vector Spaces, Fields, Finite Fields, and Galois Theory

Vector Spaces, Fields, Finite Fields, and Galois Theory. Vector spaces return as the linear structure over a field. Finite fields exist in a rigid way: for each prime power there is one, up to isomorphism. Galois theory attaches to a polynomial the group of automorphisms of its splitting field that fix the coefficients. Solvability of that group matches solvability of the polynomial by radicals. The appendices and back matter collect references and extra tools. The exercises, including the programming exercises in the early chapters, are part of the course.
