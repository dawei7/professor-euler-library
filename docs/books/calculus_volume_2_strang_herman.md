## What this book trains

Calculus, Volume 2, again from Strang and Herman’s OpenStax text, is the book of infinite processes and of integrals that need technique. Integration by parts, trigonometric integrals, trigonometric substitution, partial fractions, numerical integration and improper integrals. Then the first differential equations: direction fields, separable equations, the logistic equation, and first-order linear equations. Then sequences and series, the convergence tests, power series, and Taylor and Maclaurin series. Parametric curves, polar coordinates and the conic sections close the volume.

The opening chapters deliberately meet Volume 1’s integration and applications again. Areas, volumes, arc length, work, centers of mass, exponential growth and the hyperbolic functions are here as well as there. The repetition is the curriculum’s overlap, not a second subject. The new material begins in earnest with techniques of integration.

## Why it is worth knowing

A series is an infinite sum that might have a value. The tests, divergence, integral, comparison, alternating series, ratio and root, are criteria with hypotheses, not a menu to try at random. A power series is a polynomial of infinite degree, and it represents a function on an interval. Taylor’s theorem says how good a finite polynomial guess is. That remainder is the ancestor of every truncation error in numerical analysis.

Differential equations in this volume are the first models of change in the library. The logistic equation is growth with a ceiling. A direction field is the picture of the equation before you solve it. Numerical methods for a first step appear here and become Brin’s and Johnson’s subject later. Improper integrals are how an unbounded region can still have finite area, which is the intuition a probability density on the whole line will need.

## What it asks of the other books

Calculus Volume 1 (02), especially substitution, the fundamental theorem, and the standard derivatives. Trigonometric identities from Corral (S.03) or Stitz–Zeager (S.04), because trigonometric substitution and trigonometric integrals are algebra in disguise. Partial fractions need the rational expressions of Wallace and the decomposition preview in Stitz–Zeager. Sequences and induction in Stitz–Zeager and Hammack (01) make the series chapter a continuation.

Volume 3 (06) uses polar coordinates, parametric curves and the conics as its opening, then leaves the plane. Lebl’s differential equations (13) take over from the short differential-equations chapter. Tea Time Numerical Analysis (14) takes over from the numerical integration and the numerical direction-field remarks.

## A walk through the chapters

### 1. Integration, and Applications of Integration, reprise Volume 1

Integration, and Applications of Integration, reprise Volume 1: approximating areas, the definite integral, the fundamental theorem, formulas and net change, substitution, integrals of exponentials, logarithms and inverse trigonometric functions, then areas, slicing, shells, arc length and surface area, physical applications, moments and centers of mass, the integral view of the exponential and logarithm, growth and decay, and hyperbolic functions. If Volume 1 is solid, read these for fluency and move. If an application was skipped, this is the repair.

### 2. Techniques of Integration

Techniques of Integration is the new craft. Integration by parts, trigonometric integrals, trigonometric substitution, partial fractions, other strategies, numerical integration, and improper integrals. By parts is the product rule run backwards. Trigonometric substitution uses the identities from Corral to rationalize a square root. Partial fractions undo the addition of rational functions. Numerical integration, midpoint, trapezoid, Simpson, is what you do when no antiderivative exists in elementary functions. An improper integral takes a limit of proper integrals, and the limit may fail.

### 3. Introduction to Differential Equations

Introduction to Differential Equations. The basic vocabulary, direction fields and numerical methods, separable equations, the logistic equation, and first-order linear equations. Separable equations are an integral in disguise. A linear first-order equation has an integrating factor. The logistic equation is the model of limited growth, and it is solvable in closed form, which is a luxury later equations will not offer.

### 4. Sequences and Series

Sequences and Series. Sequences, infinite series, the divergence and integral tests, comparison tests, alternating series, and the ratio and root tests. A sequence can converge while its series diverges. The harmonic series is the famous example. An alternating series has an error estimate from its first omitted term, which is a gift: the test comes with a bound.

### 5. Power Series

Power Series. Power series as functions, their properties, Taylor and Maclaurin series, and working with Taylor series. Inside the interval of convergence you may differentiate and integrate term by term. That theorem is why a series solution of a differential equation is legitimate. Working with Taylor series includes the practical art of substitution, multiplication and the remainder, so a truncated series is an approximation with a size.

### 6. Parametric Equations and Polar Coordinates

Parametric Equations and Polar Coordinates. Parametric equations, the calculus of parametric curves, polar coordinates, area and arc length in polar coordinates, and conic sections. A parametric curve is a point moving with a parameter, often time. Polar coordinates describe a point by radius and angle, which is Corral’s polar chapter made into a place where you can integrate. The conics return as the curves whose polar equations are remarkably simple. Volume 3 starts by taking this chapter into space.
