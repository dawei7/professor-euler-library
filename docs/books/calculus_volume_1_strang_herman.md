## What this book trains

Calculus, Volume 1, the OpenStax text of Gilbert Strang and Edwin Herman, is the theory of local linear approximation and of accumulation, learned on functions of one variable. The derivative at a point is the best linear guess. The integral adds those guesses up. The fundamental theorem says the two operations undo each other.

The book is also a review of the functions it differentiates: algebraic, trigonometric, inverse, exponential and logarithmic. Limits are first an intuitive idea, then a set of laws, then the precise ε–δ definition. Derivatives become rules, rates, the chain rule, implicit differentiation, and the derivatives of the transcendental functions. Applications read the derivative as information about the shape of a graph, about related rates, about maxima, and about the error in a linear approximation.

Integration begins with approximating areas, becomes the definite integral, meets the fundamental theorem, and is practiced by substitution. Volumes, arc length, work, centers of mass, exponential growth and the hyperbolic functions show what an integral can measure.

## Why it is worth knowing

A derivative is the sentence “if the input changes a little, the output changes by about this much.” Every later model of change is that sentence with more variables, more noise, or a stricter proof. The mean value theorem is the reason a function with zero derivative on an interval is constant. L’Hôpital’s rule is a tool for limits that the later analysis book will deserve a hypothesis for. Newton’s method is the first iterative algorithm in the curriculum: follow the tangent, and watch the error.

Linear approximation is the secret bridge to linear algebra. The tangent line is a linear function. In several variables the tangent object is a linear map, a matrix. Volume 1 is where you learn to trust that replacement of a curve by its line, with a differential that records the error’s scale. Antiderivatives at the end of the applications chapter are the doorway into the integral, so the two chapters feel like one idea approached from both sides.

## What it asks of the other books

Functions, trigonometry in radians, exponentials and logarithms, from Wallace (S.01), Corral (S.03) and Stitz–Zeager (S.04). If those are fluent, this book can start. Hammack (01) is the curriculum’s recommended predecessor because the precise definition of a limit is a quantified statement, and because proofs of the limit laws are sentences of the kind Book of Proof trains. OpenStax also teaches the informal limit first, so a reader can begin the computations and return to Hammack when the ε–δ section demands it.

Volume 2 (04) continues integration, series and differential equations. Volume 3 (06) continues into several variables. Hefferon (03) does not require the integral. The curriculum places it next to this book because vectors, linear approximation and systems are about to become the same subject.

## A walk through the chapters

### 1. Functions and Graphs

Functions and Graphs reviews the cast. Functions, the basic classes, trigonometric functions, inverses, exponentials and logarithms. A reader fresh from Stitz–Zeager can treat this as a reunion. A reader who is shaky should treat it as the last chance to make composition and inverses automatic before the chain rule.

### 2. Limits

Limits. A preview of calculus, the limit of a function, the limit laws, continuity, and the precise definition. Continuity is the property that lets you pass a limit inside a function. The precise definition is the one Hammack also writes: for every tolerance on the output there is a tolerance on the input. Limits that fail are as instructive as limits that exist. A jump, an unbounded spike, or a wild oscillation are three different failures.

### 3. Derivatives

Derivatives. The definition, the derivative as a function, the differentiation rules, rates of change, trigonometric derivatives, the chain rule, derivatives of inverses, implicit differentiation, and the derivatives of exponentials and logarithms. The chain rule is composition differentiated. Implicit differentiation is the chain rule when the function is defined by an equation instead of a formula. Inverse functions bring the derivative of the logarithm and of the inverse trigonometric functions.

### 4. Applications of Derivatives

Applications of Derivatives. Related rates, linear approximations and differentials, maxima and minima, the mean value theorem, the shape of a graph, limits at infinity and asymptotes, applied optimization, L’Hôpital’s rule, Newton’s method, and antiderivatives. The mean value theorem is the engine: it turns information about the derivative into information about the function. Optimization is the derivative set equal to zero, plus a check that the critical point is the kind you wanted. Newton’s method is what you do when that equation has no closed form.

### 5. Integration

Integration. Approximating areas, the definite integral, the fundamental theorem, integration formulas and the net change theorem, substitution, and integrals of exponential, logarithmic and inverse trigonometric functions. The fundamental theorem has two faces. Differentiating an accumulation function returns the integrand. Evaluating an antiderivative between two bounds returns the definite integral. Substitution is the chain rule run backwards.

### 6. Applications of Integration

Applications of Integration. Areas between curves, volumes by slicing, cylindrical shells, arc length and surface area, physical applications, moments and centers of mass, the integral definitions that recover the logarithm and exponential, exponential growth and decay, and the hyperbolic functions. A volume by slicing is an integral of cross-sectional area. A center of mass is a weighted average, the same shape as an expectation once probability has a density. Hyperbolic functions are the even and odd combinations of exponentials, and they satisfy identities that rhyme with the trigonometric ones.
